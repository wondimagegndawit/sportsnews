# Thin cushions and roster churn strain HBCUs

No government agency, rating firm or research group publishes an average debt figure for HBCUs, so every average in this report is computed from primary filings and labeled that way. On the widest base, the 102 HBCUs in federal IPEDS data reported **$3.65 billion of long-term debt in fiscal 2024, a mean of $35.8 million and a median of $8.5 million**. On the most accurate base, 79 four-year HBCUs with a usable audit, tax filing or federal entry (mostly fiscal 2025) average **$49.8 million with a median of $22.7 million**, and the 12 SWAC members average **$58.8 million with a median of $48.4 million**. The overall position cuts against a "drowning in debt" argument: HBCUs owe less than comparable non-HBCUs, and their fragility comes from thin endowments, deferred maintenance, reliance on public money and athletics departments that run on subsidy. On transfers, no complete SWAC count exists in any source the research team could open. The best evidence is that **33 SWAC football players moved up to FBS after the 2024 season and at least 39 after 2025**, and that in men's basketball **only 51 of the 185 players who appeared in 2024-25 came back, 44 played for another school the next season, and 29 of those 44 left after a single season**. The Protect College Sports Act (S. 4668) would allow one free undergraduate transfer, a sit-out year for a second, and a five-year eligibility clock. It **passed the Senate 77-22 on September 28, 2026** and, as of November 27, sits at the House desk with a floor vote promised for the first week of December and a hard stop on January 3, 2027. The SWAC, the MEAC and UNCF already support it; the organized opposition is the Congressional Black Caucus, the NAACP and athlete groups, which changes who the argument has to persuade.

## No published HBCU debt average exists; computed means run from $36 million to $59 million

The research found no average or median HBCU institutional debt figure from NCES, GAO, UNCF, the Thurgood Marshall College Fund, Urban, Brookings, Moody's, S&P or Fitch. The only published sector-level study, Ithaka S+R's 2022 report, presents charts and no headline average, and concludes that HBCUs "do not appear to deviate from their non-HBCU peers significantly in terms of debt per FTE" ([Ithaka S+R](https://sr.ithaka.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Ithaka-SR_-BorrowingDuringCrisis_Aug312022.pdf)). An AAUP article on HBCU debt likewise gives no totals and points readers to IPEDS and tax filings ([AAUP](https://www.aaup.org/article/battling-institutional-debt-hbcus)). Every average below is therefore **a computation made for this report, not a published statistic**. All figures are institutional debt; none includes student loans, pensions or retiree health obligations.

Two computations answer the question on different bases. The first is a uniform census: the NCES IPEDS Finance files for fiscal 2024 (files dated December 30, 2025), joined to the federal HBCU flag, cover all 102 HBCUs, 51 public and 51 private nonprofit. They report **$3.650 billion** in long-term debt, a mean of **$35.8 million** and a median of **$8.5 million**; 31 schools report zero, and among the 71 reporting any debt the mean is $51.4 million and the median $27.3 million ([NCES IPEDS public file](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/complete-data-files/F2324_F1A.zip); [private nonprofit file](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/complete-data-files/F2324_F2.zip); [HBCU flag](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/complete-data-files/HD2024.zip)). The second is a school-by-school build from the best public document for each institution: audited financial statements for 49 schools, Form 990 balance sheets for 11 private colleges, and IPEDS entries for 19 four-year publics. It defines debt as bonds, notes and loans, drawn credit lines and lease or financed-purchase obligations. That set of 79 totals **$3.93 billion, a mean of $49.8 million and a median of $22.7 million**; 46 of the 79 figures are fiscal 2025 and 33 are fiscal 2024, so it blends two years ([Federal Audit Clearinghouse](https://app.fac.gov/dissemination/search/); [ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/)).

## HBCUs owe less than their peers, and the weakness is the cushion behind the debt

The evidence does not support a claim that HBCUs carry heavier debt loads than other colleges. In the fiscal 2024 IPEDS data (computed for this report), four-year public HBCUs average $52.0 million in debt against $215.6 million at non-HBCU publics, and private HBCUs average $27.3 million against $105.0 million. Debt as a share of assets is lower at HBCUs in both sectors. The one leverage measure on which public HBCUs look worse is debt against endowment: their median debt is 1.17 times their endowment, compared with 0.77 at non-HBCU publics, and their total liabilities equal half their assets.

| Four-year group (FY2024, computed from IPEDS) | Schools | Median debt | Debt ÷ assets | Liabilities ÷ assets | Debt ÷ endowment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public HBCUs | 42 | $38.4M | 13.9% | 50.4% | 1.17 |
| Public non-HBCUs | 712 | $53.2M | 17.5% | 43.7% | 0.77 |
| Private HBCUs | 50 | $0.2M | 0.5% | 17.5% | 0.01 |
| Private non-HBCUs | 1,438 | $12.1M | 11.6% | 23.4% | 0.32 |

Low private-HBCU debt partly reflects a federal discharge and constrained access to credit, an interpretation consistent with what Ithaka and GAO report about borrowing barriers, and it should not be read as strength. In 2021 the Education Department discharged about **$1.6 billion owed by 45 HBCUs** under its Capital Financing Program, a published figure that works out to roughly $36 million per school by simple division ([AAUP](https://www.aaup.org/article/battling-institutional-debt-hbcus)). Schools have re-borrowed quickly through the same program: Alabama A&M went from $13.0 million in 2021 to $86.6 million in 2025, and Tuskegee, Dillard and Florida A&M each added tens of millions in a single year. The department's fiscal 2027 budget justification shows **$830.3 million outstanding on 25 loans in 2025**, projects $1.44 billion by 2027, and describes borrowers as "some of the Nation's most vulnerable institutions of higher education" that "cannot access traditional funding sources at reasonable interest rates" ([Education Department FY2027 justification](https://www.ed.gov/media/document/fy-2027-congressional-justification-historically-black-college-and-university-capital-financing-program-account-113548.pdf)). The department's program web page reportedly cites a larger balance of about $1.7 billion; the research team could not open that page and could not reconcile the two figures. Moody's rated only eight HBCUs as of October 2024 ([Moody's](https://ma.moodys.com/rs/961-KCJ-308/images/Sector_In-Depth-Higher-Education-US-Favorable.pdf?version=0)).

The stability case rests on what stands behind the debt. HBCUs were 1.4% of institutions reporting to NACUBO in fiscal 2025 and held 0.3% of the $953.7 billion in endowment assets ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/hbcu-campus-and-community-development/)). The average public HBCU endowment was $41 million in a November 2025 report ([TMCF](https://tmcf.org/landmark-report-public-hbcus-power-u-s-competitiveness-and-economic-mobility-yet-face-severe-funding-disparities/)), while four-year public HBCUs reported an average deferred maintenance backlog of $96 million in May 2024, up 43% from GAO's 2018 survey; that series comes from an advocacy group's member survey ([TMCF fact sheet](https://tmcf.org/wp-content/uploads/HBCU-Infrastructure-Needs-Fact-Sheet-2024-Updated-Finals-2.pdf)). Public HBCUs drew 54% of revenue from government sources against 38% at other publics, in data that run only through about 2015 ([ACE/UNCF](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstream/handle/10919/89184/PublicPrivateHbcus.pdf)). Federal officials estimated in 2023 that 16 states underfunded their land-grant HBCUs by more than $12 billion from 1987 to 2020; six SWAC members account for about $5.3 billion of that by this report's addition, and no state has paid the federal figure ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/states-urged-by-biden-administration-to-rectify-underfunding-of-land-grant-hbcus/)). That number is a federal estimate built on a per-student comparison, Tennessee has not accepted it, and it should be described that way.

Recent failures show the mechanism, and they complicate a debt-centered story. Saint Augustine's University filed Chapter 11 in April 2026 listing $50 million to $100 million in liabilities, including $14.4 million owed to the IRS, after borrowing $7 million at 24% ([ABC11](https://abc11.com/post/saint-augustines-university-files-chapter-11-bankruptcy/18986410/); [The Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/articles/struggling-hbcu-st-augustines-takes-a-high-rate-loan)). Tennessee State needed $43 million in emergency state cash to make payroll in November 2024 ([Tennessee Lookout](https://tennesseelookout.com/2024/11/15/tennessee-officials-approved-43-million-in-emergency-funds-for-tsu/)). Fisk's fiscal 2025 audit records a covenant breach that gave a lender the right to call its debt, and Talladega's debt is about 2.85 times its annual revenue ([Fisk audit](https://app.fac.gov/dissemination/report/pdf/2025-06-GSAFAC-0000424801); [Talladega audit](https://app.fac.gov/dissemination/report/pdf/2025-06-GSAFAC-0000410936)). The common trigger in the reporting is enrollment swings and operating deficits hitting thin reserves, with small, expensive short-term borrowing doing the damage, and auditors or accreditors often cite control failures as well. Inside the SWAC, Florida A&M and Texas Southern both carry material-weakness findings for fiscal 2025 ([Florida Auditor General](https://flauditor.gov/pages/pdf_files/2027-013.pdf)). Critics of the underfunding argument point to findings like these, so an honest brief acknowledges them.

Athletics is where this fragility meets the bill. The 12 SWAC departments spent a combined $153.6 million in 2024-25, with a median of **$13.0 million**, about half the FCS median, and all twelve rank in the bottom 31 of 131 FCS programs by spending (computed from the federal [EADA data file](https://ope.ed.gov/athletics/api/dataFiles/file?fileName=EADA_2024-2025.zip)). In the last complete public subsidy table, fiscal 2022, **66.7% of SWAC public-school athletics revenue came from student fees and institutional or state support** (computed from the [USA Today database](https://sportsdata.usatoday.com/ncaa/finances)). Grambling's audited fiscal 2025 athletics deficit was $5.08 million ([KSLA](https://www.ksla.com/2026/05/11/deficit-more-than-5-million-discovered-audit-grambling-athletics-dept/)). The SWAC was not a defendant in the House settlement yet is being charged $34 million over 10 years, with $4.14 million withheld from its NCAA distributions this year, about $345,000 per school by simple division ([HBCU Sports](https://hbcusports.com/2026/11/27/swac-sets-another-conference-revenue-record/)). The 2025-26 revenue-sharing cap of about $20.5 million per school exceeds the largest SWAC athletics budget, Alabama State's $17.9 million ([Knight Commission](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/KnightCommissionBrief_HousevNCAA_182025.pdf)).

In football, the Houston Defender printed a HERO Sports list of about 33 SWAC players who transferred up to FBS after the 2024 season, nine of them to Power 4 schools ([Houston Defender](https://defendernetwork.com/sports/college-sports/swac-football-transfer-portal/)). For the cycle after the 2025 season, combining FCS Football Central's tracker with 247Sports team pages yields **at least 39 SWAC-to-FBS moves**, again nine to Power 4 programs, plus 40 lateral moves to other FCS schools, for **at least 79 players landing at a new Division I school**: 49% up to FBS, 33% to FCS schools outside the conference and 18% to a SWAC rival ([FCS-to-FBS tracker](https://www.si.com/college/fcs/fcs-football-recruiting/2026-fcs-to-fbs-college-football-transfer-portal-tracker); [FCS-to-FCS tracker](https://www.si.com/college/fcs/fcs-football-recruiting/2026-fcs-to-fcs-college-football-transfer-portal-tracker)). These are floors. They omit everyone who entered the portal and went to Division II, NAIA, junior college or nowhere, and the two years were compiled by different methods, so the rise from 33 to 39 is suggestive. At least eight of the 52 players on the 2025 All-SWAC first and second teams left for FBS in January 2026 ([SWAC release](https://swac.org/news/2025/12/1/swac-announces-2025-football-postseason-awards.aspx)).

Men's basketball gives the clearest year-to-year picture because every player's game appearances are traceable in ESPN's data. This tally measures actual movement, not portal entry ([ESPN team statistics](https://site.web.api.espn.com/apis/site/v2/sports/basketball/mens-college-basketball/teams/2296/athletes/statistics?season=2026)). Of 185 players who appeared for SWAC teams in 2024-25, 51 returned, carrying 25.5% of the league's minutes; 44 played for a different team in 2025-26; and 90 appeared nowhere, a group that mixes exhausted eligibility with players who dropped to untracked levels. The returning-minutes share has fallen on a consistent method from 43.5% entering 2023-24 to 29.3%, 25.5% and an early-season 16.8% entering 2026-27. Alcorn State returned none of the 11 players who appeared in 2025-26, a finding from this tally alone that should be checked against Alcorn's official rosters before publication. The only published SWAC count is HBCU Gameday's 31 public portal declarations as of April 6, 2026, when all four of the league's individual award winners had signaled entry ([HBCU Gameday](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/04/07/transfer-portal-draws-top-hbcu-talent-again/)). ESPN data show one of the four, Player of the Year Daeshun Ruffin, back at Jackson State this season.

On departures after one year, no published count exists for any SWAC sport. By the same ESPN tally, **124 of the 185 men's players in 2024-25 were in their first season at their school, 86 of those did not return, and 29 played for a different school the very next season**; the 2025-26 cohort stands at 122 first-season players, 95 not back and 20 already playing elsewhere. The 29 and 20 are the firm one-and-done transfer counts. The 86 and 95 overstate voluntary exits because many first-season players are seniors or graduate transfers using a final year. Football has no systematic count, only pointed cases: of 70 FBS transfers who joined four SWAC programs for 2024, at least two left for FBS schools after one season and two more are on their third SWAC school in three seasons ([HBCU Legends](https://www.si.com/college/hbcu/football/swac-teams-and-tennessee-state-fbs-to-fcs-transfers-mosley)).

Three facts keep this from being a simple story of bigger programs raiding the SWAC. Men's basketball leavers rarely move up: across three cycles only 2 of 96 who played elsewhere went to a power-conference team, while about a quarter moved to a SWAC rival. The SWAC imports as heavily as it exports: its football programs took 51 players from other FCS schools in the 2026 cycle, Prairie View A&M signed more than 70 new players with eight from high school, and 48% of 2025-26 SWAC men's basketball players had already played for two or more schools ([HBCU Gameday](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/02/03/prairie-view-am-transfer-portal-hbcu-football/)). And nationally FBS portal entries fell about 23% in January 2026, which NCAA President Charlie Baker credited to revenue sharing ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/college-football-transfers-down-23-after-schools-started-paying-players/)). The strongest independent support for limits is academic. NCAA data released November 11, 2026 show Black athletes graduating at 82% with no transfers, 74% with one and 62% with more than one, and a 21-point drop in FCS football between non-transfers and multiple transfers ([AP](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/ap-top-news/2026/11/11/ncaa-warns-multiple-transfers-tied-to-sharply-lower-graduation-rates-in-college-football)). The NCAA released that data while lobbying for the bill, and in men's basketball one-time transfers graduate at a higher rate (80%) than non-transfers (74%), so the case is against repeated transfers specifically.

## The bill permits one free transfer and a five-year clock, and its fate turns on a December House vote

The Protect College Sports Act of 2026 is S. 4668, sponsored by Senator Ted Cruz with Senator Maria Cantwell as lead Democratic cosponsor. It is a different bill from the stalled House SCORE Act (H.R. 4312) and from H.R. 5076, the similarly named "Protection of College Sports Act" ([GovInfo bill status](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/s/BILLSTATUS-119s4668.xml)).

On transfers, Section 112 requires schools, conferences and associations to "permit a student athlete to transfer from one four-year institution to another four-year institution (1) once without losing or delaying eligibility," a second time "with a loss of eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports during the first academic year following the transfer," and additionally without penalty upon discontinuation of the athlete's sport, "the departure of the head coach," sexual assault or harassment by someone associated with the school, or "the student athlete pursuing a graduate degree" ([Senate-passed text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4668es/xml/BILLS-119s4668es.xml)). On eligibility, Section 113 sets "a maximum of 5 calendar years" starting at the earlier of initial full-time enrollment or the academic year after the athlete's 19th birthday, with exceptions for pregnancy, religious missions and military service; it bars professional athletes and lets associations adopt uniform absence rules for serious injury. Section 118 gives antitrust immunity for enforcing those sections, Section 119 confines transfer and eligibility challenges to federal court, Section 121 preempts state laws on transfers, eligibility and NIL, and Section 122 states that the title "is neutral on, and does nothing to alter, employee or non-employee status." The research team did not locate an effective date for Sections 112 and 113.

The legal effect is to restore power the NCAA lost. A December 2023 injunction and a May 2024 settlement with the Justice Department and ten states permanently barred the NCAA from making multi-time transfers sit out, and unlimited immediate eligibility remains the rule until a statute changes it ([Ohio Attorney General](https://ohioattorneygeneral.gov/Media/News-Releases/May-2024/AG-Yost-and-Allies-Score-Major-Win-for-College-Ath)). The eligibility clock mirrors the age-based rule the NCAA adopted in June 2026, which drew new lawsuits within a day ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/article/new-ncaa-eligibility-policy-hasnt-slowed-lawsuits-its-triggered-more/)).

The status on November 27, 2026 is passed by one chamber and stalled at the door of the other. The Senate Commerce Committee reported the bill 19-9 in June, and the Senate passed it 77-22 on September 28 ([Senate roll call 250](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1192/vote_119_2_00250.xml)). The House received it October 5 and holds it at the desk, the last official action. The House Rules Committee has posted an amended text, Rules Committee Print 119-42, with 26 amendments filed by a November 25 deadline; the transfer, eligibility and employment sections are word for word the same as the Senate's ([House Rules Committee](https://rules.house.gov/bill/119/s-4668)). The House text changes conference-size and revenue-cap provisions instead. One filed amendment, from Rep. Cohen (D-TN), would limit the free transfer to moves before an athlete's third year. Republican leaders said on November 19 they will bring the bill to the floor in the first week of December while conceding it "faces real challenges in the House from many factions of our conference" and that Democrats are "outright opposing it" ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/house-gop-protect-college-sports-act/)). Republicans hold a 218-214 margin, any House change requires a second Senate vote, and Democrats, who won both chambers on November 3, take over when the new Congress is sworn in on January 3. President Trump says he will sign it. Cruz predicts "an overwhelming bipartisan vote" in the House, while Rep. Jeff Van Drew, a cosponsor of the House companion, rates the chances as low ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/sports/4813964/trump-gop-push-college-sports-bill/)).

## The opposition is Black lawmakers, and the transfer rule cuts both ways for the SWAC

The premise that HBCU leaders need persuading is out of date. SWAC Commissioner Charles McClelland wrote to Congress on June 10, 2026 that "The SWAC supports the Protect College Sports Act," and the MEAC, CIAA and SIAC are reported as supporters ([Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/19/protect-college-sports-act-hbcu/)). UNCF endorsed the bill in August ([HBCU Gameday](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/08/06/college-sports-bill-has-uncf-congressional-black-caucus-on-opposite-sides/)). The organized opposition is the Congressional Black Caucus, which asked the sponsors on June 3 to pause the bill while college sports leaders stay silent on "attacks on Black political representation," and condemned Senate passage on September 29 because the bill grants no employment rights, restricts athletes' private legal action and does not guarantee HBCU funding ([CBC letter](https://cbc.house.gov/uploadedfiles/cbc_letter_regarding_protect_college_sports_act.pdf); [CBC statement](https://cbc.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=3237)). The NAACP, the National Urban League and the National College Players Association have also criticized or opposed it, and all four Black Caucus senators voted no ([Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/college-football/article/explainer-everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-protect-college-sports-act-including-whether-bill-has-chance-to-pass-132539375.html)). Some House Republicans oppose it from the other side because it does not ban employee status. HBCU commissioners and the Black Caucus want opposite things on that question: the commissioners called athlete employment an "existential threat" in a May 2026 letter, while the caucus objects that the bill fails to grant it ([HBCU Gameday](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/05/13/hbcu-conferences-ask-congress-for-nil-action/)).

The bill does little for the balance sheets described above, and nothing for institutional debt. Its HBCU title authorizes $180 million a year for fiscal 2027 through 2032 in competitive broadcast and broadband grants, but the money is authorized, not appropriated, and the advocacy group Athletes.org calls it a "bargaining chip" ([HBCU Gameday](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/09/29/hbcus-hoping-to-benefit-from-pcsa-but-future-uncertain/)). Division I HBCUs would take on new medical-coverage mandates, including five years of post-eligibility injury costs, backstopped by an NCAA hardship fund of at least $60 million for schools under $20 million in athletics revenue. The bill contains no subsidy relief for low-revenue schools. The one point of contact with debt is unsettled. CBS reports that "a path to student loan forgiveness for HBCU attendees" is under discussion as a sweetener, and UNCF's letter praised work on forgiving remaining Capital Financing Program debt, yet a filed amendment from Rep. French Hill (R-AR) would add HBCU infrastructure grants explicitly "without its capital loan forgiveness provisions." A financial-stability argument for the bill therefore runs through cost predictability and competitive order, since the text offers no direct relief.

The transfer section would also bind the SWAC's own roster model. A fifth of 2025-26 SWAC men's basketball players had already played for three or more schools, and repeat undergraduate moves of that kind would carry a sit-out year unless an exception applied. The head-coach exception would leave the worst SWAC exoduses untouched, because Grambling's 2025 losses and the 2026 departures at Alabama State and Jackson State all followed a coach leaving. The graduate exception keeps both the drop-down pipeline and upward graduate moves open. HBCU Gameday's own assessment is that "it is uncertain whether these rules will help smaller programs keep talent, given how much HBCU football relies on the transfer portal" ([HBCU Gameday](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/09/29/hbcus-hoping-to-benefit-from-pcsa-but-future-uncertain/)). SWAC coaches are split in the same way. Grambling's Mickey Joseph says "the only ones who lose in this are the SWAC and MEAC" and wants a buyout paid to the losing school, which the bill does not provide ([Houston Defender](https://defendernetwork.com/sports/college-sports/swac-football-transfer-portal/)). Prairie View's Tremaine Jackson says of building through transfers, "I don't think we can be successful and keep our jobs with a bunch of 18-year-olds" ([HBCU Sports](https://hbcusports.com/2026/01/13/prairie-views-tremaine-jackson-says-the-quiet-part-out-loud-hes-not-wrong/)). Both quotes were captured as short excerpts and should be checked against the full text before printing. No statement from McClelland on transfers was found.

## Conclusion

The numbers support a narrower argument than the one intended, and a stronger one. HBCUs are undercapitalized: the median school owes between about $9 million and $48 million depending on the measure, less than its peers, against an endowment and reserve base too small to absorb one bad enrollment year, a $5 million athletics deficit or a six-figure settlement deduction. Framing the case around heavy debt invites a rebuttal from the same federal data used to compute the averages. Framing it around thin margins, two-thirds-subsidized athletics budgets and rosters where two of every three men's basketball players are new each year holds up, provided the one-year figures are stated as 29 confirmed next-season transfers out of 124 first-year players, with the higher non-return figures explained.

The audience matters as much as the evidence. HBCU conferences and UNCF are already in the yes column, so a piece aimed at them argues a settled point. The votes that decide the bill in the next three weeks belong to House Democrats and the Congressional Black Caucus, whose objections concern athlete employment, the right to sue, and money that is authorized without being guaranteed. A persuasive case has to meet those objections directly and concede that the transfer rule would cost SWAC programs some of the multi-time transfers they now recruit. If the bill dies on January 3, the next Congress is likely to weigh employment and bargaining rights more heavily, which is the outcome HBCU commissioners have said they fear most.
