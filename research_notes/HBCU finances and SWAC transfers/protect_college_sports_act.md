## 1. Which bill is the "Protect College Sports Act", and how does it relate to the similarly named measures?

### Takeaway
The name belongs to **S. 4668, the "Protect College Sports Act of 2026" (PCSA)**, a Senate bill by Commerce Chairman Ted Cruz (R-TX) with Ranking Member Maria Cantwell (D-WA) as lead Democratic cosponsor, introduced June 2, 2026 and passed by the Senate 77-22 on September 28, 2026. It is a different bill from the House SCORE Act (H.R. 4312, 2025), which stalled and which the PCSA has effectively replaced as the live vehicle; it also must not be confused with H.R. 5076, the "Protection of College Sports Act" (2025).

### Cited Findings
- S. 4668's official short title is "Protect College Sports Act of 2026"; sponsor Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX); introduced in the Senate June 2, 2026 and referred to Commerce, Science, and Transportation. — [GovInfo bill status data, S. 4668](https://www.govinfo.gov/bulkdata/BILLSTATUS/119/s/BILLSTATUS-119s4668.xml)
- The bill's long title: "To protect the name, image, and likeness rights of, and provide protections for, student athletes and to promote fair competition among intercollegiate athletics, and for other purposes." — [Wikipedia, Protect College Sports Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protect_College_Sports_Act); same wording in [House Rules Committee Print 119-42](https://rules.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-rules.house.gov/files/documents/rcp_s4668_xml.pdf)

### Inferences
- The PCSA is the Senate's bipartisan successor to the SCORE Act effort, not a renamed SCORE Act. Any 2025 material about "HBCU commissioners support federal legislation" refers to SCORE; 2026 material from June onward refers to the PCSA.
- Because the House is amending S. 4668 itself (see section 2), H.R. 9137 is unlikely to move separately.

### Gaps
- The July 24, 2025 executive order "Saving College Sports", the Senate Democrats' SAFE Act (Cantwell/Booker/Blumenthal, 2025) and the 2025 Cruz discussion draft were named in the assignment but not re-verified in this session. One HBCU outlet describes an "April 2026 executive order, 'Saving College Sports'", which appears to blur the July 2025 order and EO 14400; not resolved.

## 2. Bill number, sponsors, legislative history, current status (Nov. 27, 2026), next steps and prospects

### Takeaway
S. 4668 has passed the Senate and is pending in the House, which has not yet voted. House GOP leaders have posted an amended text (Rules Committee Print 119-42) and say they will bring it to the floor in the first week of December; any House change requires a second Senate vote before the 119th Congress ends on January 3, 2027, after which Democrats take control. Passage is genuinely uncertain: Republicans hold only 218-214, leadership concedes "real challenges" inside its own conference, and says Democrats are "outright opposing" it.

### Inferences
- Sequence still required: Rules Committee meeting and rule; House floor passage of the amended text; Senate concurrence (which needs 60 votes for cloture unless done by consent, against a pool of 22 prior no votes); presidential signature. All inside roughly three legislative weeks that also contain a government-funding deadline.
- With a four-seat GOP margin, 21 Republicans on record against the conference cap, and others demanding a no-employee clause, the bill likely needs a meaningful number of Democratic votes. That makes the roughly 60-member CBC, which is on record against it, one of the pivotal blocs. - If the bill dies on Jan. 3, the next Congress is Democratic-controlled, where athlete-employment and collective-bargaining demands would carry more weight; that changes what HBCU leaders could expect on the employment question they care most about.

## 3. What the bill would do

### Takeaway
The PCSA writes a national rulebook into federal law (one free undergraduate transfer, a five-year eligibility clock, a ban on pros returning, a codified House-settlement cap plus a retention fund, agent rules, health and scholarship guarantees), gives the NCAA, conferences and schools antitrust immunity for enforcing those specific rules, preempts state NIL, transfer and eligibility laws, and stays neutral on employment. It contains several small-school and HBCU-specific provisions, the largest being a Title III HBCU sports-media grant authorization of $180 million a year for fiscal 2027-2032, which is authorized but not appropriated.

### Cited Findings
All quotations in this section are from the Senate-passed text unless noted: [S. 4668 as engrossed by the Senate](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4668es/xml/BILLS-119s4668es.xml). The House print keeps Secs. 112, 113 and 122 unchanged.

**Transfers (Sec. 112, "Transfer protections")**
- Institutions, conferences and associations "shall permit a student athlete to transfer from one four-year institution to another four-year institution (1) once without losing or delaying eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports; (2) except as provided in paragraph (3), a second time with a loss of eligibility to participate in intercollegiate sports during the first academic year following the transfer; and (3) additionally, without losing or delaying eligibility ... upon (A) discontinuation of a sport in which the student athlete competes; (B) the departure of the head coach of the student athlete's varsity sports team; (C) sexual assault or harassment of the student athlete by an individual associated with the student athlete's institution; or (D) the student athlete pursuing a graduate degree."
- Sec. 118(a)(6) makes it not unlawful under the antitrust laws to "enforce or comply with" Sec. 112, and Sec. 118(b)(3)(B) lets an association or conference "restrict the eligibility of a student athlete who ... has transferred to an institution in a manner that does not comply with section 112."
- Sec. 113(e): no association or conference may require a transfer from a two-year institution to meet academic standards "more stringent" than those for four-year transfers.
- Sec. 117 covers recruitment and tampering; the NCAA says the bill "empowers the NCAA to set and enforce transfer windows and tampering rules." The Senate text contains no specific transfer-window dates (word search). — [NCAA, Protect College Sports Act page](https://www.ncaa.org/protect-college-sports-act/)
- Sponsors' framing: "An enforceable transfer framework. One free undergrad transfer." — [Senate Commerce PCSA fact sheet](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/PCSA-Fact-Sheet.pdf)
- Press description: this "restores the NCAA's 2021 rule, which courts later replaced with unlimited transfers." — [Salt Lake Tribune, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/)

**Eligibility (Sec. 113)**
- Eligible if the individual "is a student athlete", "meets uniform academic standards", "is not a professional athlete", and complies with rules restricting eligibility for violations of law.
- "a student athlete is eligible to compete in intercollegiate athletic competition for a Division I or Division II institution ... for a maximum of 5 calendar years beginning on, whichever occurs first (A) the beginning of the academic year following the 19th birthday of the student athlete; or (B) the date the student athlete initially enrolls full time at an institution."
- Clock exceptions: pregnancy, religious mission, active-duty military service, and "Other periods of absence, which may include serious athletic injury or medical condition, adopted by rule or bylaw by an intercollegiate athletic association that apply uniformly to all student athletes."
- Associations and conferences may restrict eligibility for illegal or performance-enhancing drug use and for sports wagering or "sports-related event contracts"; institutions may enforce their general codes of conduct.
- Carve-outs from "professional athlete": basketball players' competition before age 19 or first full-time enrollment, and prize money in sports other than football or basketball paid only by an event sponsor.
- Sec. 123(b)(2)(A): nothing in the applicability section shall "allow a student athlete eligibility ... for more than a total of 5 years." Pending cases are otherwise preserved, and Sec. 123(c) preserves claims for money damages for conduct before enactment.
- Sponsors' summary: "Max. five years of playing time beginning at enrollment in college/age 19. No pro athletes"; "five year/age 24 cap". — [Senate Commerce PCSA fact sheet](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/PCSA-Fact-Sheet.pdf)

**Limits on litigation over transfers and eligibility (Secs. 118-119)**
- Sec. 118(a): antitrust immunity for enforcing or complying with Secs. 112 (transfers), 113 (eligibility), 114-115 (compensation cap), 117 (recruiting/tampering), 103 (agents), 110 (coaching transitions) and certain NIL provisions; Sec. 118(b) covers fines, postseason bans, athlete ineligibility and agent decertification. The association gets the immunity only if it has adopted rules implementing those sections.
- Sec. 119: an athlete may sue over Sec. 112 or Sec. 113 only by alleging the association, conference or school "has not complied with" the statutory transfer or eligibility standard, and only "in an appropriate district court of the United States" (i.e., federal court, not state court). "The protection from antitrust liability set forth in section 118 shall not be limited by a private right of action."
- The NCAA's summary: the bill "grants a limited cause of action, so eligibility and transfer challenges can only be heard in federal court." — [NCAA, Protect College Sports Act page](https://www.ncaa.org/protect-college-sports-act/)

**Preemption (Sec. 121)**
- No state may maintain a law that conflicts with the title or that "governs, regulates, or invalidates policies or rules of an institution, a conference, or an intercollegiate athletic association that regulates (A) the compensation to a student athlete ... for the use of their name, image, or likeness ...; (B) transfers of student athletes between institutions; or (C)" the core eligibility provisions of Sec. 113. Civil-rights, tort, contract, consumer-protection and similar state laws are preserved unless they conflict.

**Employment (Sec. 122)**
- Entire section: "This title is neutral on, and does nothing to alter, employee or non-employee status for student athletes."
- Cruz said collective bargaining was "omitted by design to allow for state-specific approaches." — [USA Today via Yahoo Sports, Nov. 19, 2026](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/protect-college-sports-act-looked-184055045.html)

**Revenue sharing and the cap (Secs. 100, 114, 115)**
- "Revenue share cap" means "the Benefits Pool Limit set forth in the Injunctive Relief Settlement Agreement approved by the court in In Re College Athlete NIL Litigation, No. 20-cv-03919 (N.D. Cal. June 6, 2025), or as modified" under the settlement's amendment clause. Schools, conferences, staff, volunteers and "associated entities" may not provide compensation that "would circumvent or result in the institution exceeding the revenue share cap."
- Retention fund: a school "may exceed the revenue share cap by not more than $22,500,000 per academic year for the purpose of retaining" athletes who "have spent at least one full competitive season at the institution", plus "up to an additional $5,000,000" tied to spending on non-revenue (women's and Olympic) sports; available for nine years and only to schools meeting academic benchmarks.
- Press arithmetic: 2026-27 cap about $21.3 million, rising to as much as $48.8 million per school with the retention and non-revenue pools. — [Washington Examiner, Nov. 27, 2026](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/sports/4813964/trump-gop-push-college-sports-bill/); [CBS Sports, Nov. 19, 2026](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/house-gop-protect-college-sports-act/)
- Third-party NIL deals must be for a "valid business purpose"; multimedia-rights holders, sponsors, apparel companies and vendors must certify the school is not the source of the money (Sec. 114(c)-(d)). No compensation to prospects before enrollment (Sec. 114(a)(1)(C)).
- Schools with more than $80 million in athletics revenue may not pay a coach or general manager more than $500,000 from sources other than "college sports revenue or a donation or contribution to the athletic department" (Sec. 114(e)).

**Agents (Secs. 102-103)**
- Caps agent NIL fees at 5 percent of the endorsement contract; agency contracts cannot outlast college eligibility; agents must register; the NCAA maintains a public registry and may decertify or fine agents. — [Salt Lake Tribune, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/); [Senate Commerce PCSA fact sheet](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/PCSA-Fact-Sheet.pdf)

**Olympic, women's and non-revenue sports (Sec. 125)**
- Division I conferences and associations "shall not reduce" the minimum number of teams, participants or contests required for Division I or FBS membership.
- Schools with at least $80 million in athletics revenue must "offer and maintain at least as many total grant-in-aid opportunities and total roster spots for non-revenue generating intercollegiate sports programs ... as the member institution provided during academic year 2024-2025" for nine years, with hardship waivers; schools between $50 million and $80 million have the same duty for four years with broader exemptions. Schools under $50 million (which includes every SWAC and MEAC member on the revenue figures cited below) are not covered by the roster/scholarship maintenance mandate.

**Health and scholarship protections (Secs. 105-108)**
- Division I schools must cover athletes' out-of-pocket costs for sports injuries, an independent second opinion, catastrophic coverage above $90,000, an exit physical, and five years of post-eligibility out-of-pocket costs for sports injuries (Sec. 106(a)).
- The association must run a fund of at least $60 million (rising to a $100 million ceiling) to help "a Division I institution that generates less than $20,000,000 in total annual athletics revenue" meet the five-year post-eligibility mandate "in the event of demonstrated financial hardship", and to cover long-term conditions such as CTE (Sec. 106(b)).
- Ten years of post-eligibility scholarship access. — [Washington Examiner, Nov. 27, 2026](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/sports/4813964/trump-gop-push-college-sports-bill/)

**Governance and study**
- Sec. 111: athlete representation on association governing boards; one outlet reports athletes would be at least one-third of NCAA-type boards and rulemaking committees. — [HBCU Gameday, Sept. 29, 2026](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/09/29/hbcus-hoping-to-benefit-from-pcsa-but-future-uncertain/)
- Sec. 126: associations must give "adequate representation" on governing and rulemaking bodies to "mid-sized" conferences, defined as those generating "less than $500,000,000 in total annual revenue". The SWAC and MEAC fall under that definition on any plausible reading.
- Sec. 116: a legislative-branch Commission on the Future of College Athletics, with "1 member [who] shall be a representative of a historically Black college or university, appointed by the majority leader of the Senate" and one appointed by the Speaker, plus two mid-sized-conference representatives. The commission can recommend changes to the revenue-sharing figures.

**Title II, sports broadcasting**
- Conferences may pool football media rights in a single "covered entity" with Sports Broadcasting Act antitrust protection if at least 75 percent of FBS schools join; pooled revenue must be distributed by a formula that includes a guaranteed minimum, a 15 percent equal share among FBS football schools, and a performance share; participation is optional. — [Salt Lake Tribune, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/); [Data Driven HBCU, May 27, 2026](https://datadrivenhbcu.com/2026/05/27/what-does-the-protect-college-sports-act-mean-for-division-i-hbcus/)
- Power-conference limits: conferences above $700 million in revenue (Senate text; $600 million in the House print) face merger and expansion restrictions, with a membership cap of 20 (Senate) or 22 (House print) and a three-year independence requirement for schools switching power leagues during the first six years. — [Salt Lake Tribune, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/); [Washington Examiner, Nov. 27, 2026](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/sports/4813964/trump-gop-push-college-sports-bill/)
- Mid-season coaching-move restrictions (Sec. 110, the "Lane Kiffin Rule") apply to FBS football personnel. — [Salt Lake Tribune, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/)

**Title III, "HBCU Sports Media and Connectivity Program"**
- The Assistant Secretary of Commerce for Telecommunications and Information (the NTIA head), in consultation with the Secretary of Education, "shall award grants to eligible entities, on a competitive basis, to support long-term improvements to broadband, information technology, media infrastructure, including infrastructure for the production, transmission, and distribution of live coverage of intercollegiate athletic events, and local journalism for student learning." Grants run two to five years. "Eligible entity" means a "part B institution" (an HBCU under the Higher Education Act) or an institution listed in HEA section 326(e)(1). The title also defines "HBCU athletic conference" as "a conference the membership of which consists predominantly of part B institutions."
- Sec. 307: "There is authorized to be appropriated to carry out this title $180,000,000 for each of fiscal years 2027 through 2032" (up to $1.08 billion over six years).
- The title is also called the Ignite HBCUs Sports and Media Act and resembles the IGNITE HBCU Excellence Act of Reps. Alma Adams and French Hill; a congressional source told CBS Sports it could help win votes from members representing HBCUs. — [HBCU Legends/SI via Yahoo Sports, Aug. 5, 2026](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/title-iii-five-questions-hbcu-112345127.html)
- Critics' caveat: the money is not appropriated; a future Congress would have to fund it and HBCUs would have to compete for grants. Athletes.org calls it a "bargaining chip" for the antitrust protections. — [HBCU Gameday, Sept. 29, 2026](https://hbcugameday.com/2026/09/29/hbcus-hoping-to-benefit-from-pcsa-but-future-uncertain/)

**House-print addition relevant to subsidies**
- An institution "with an average annual media rights revenue of $50,000,000 or more ... will not ... use student fees to support intercollegiate athletic programs." — [Rules Committee Print 119-42](https://rules.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-rules.house.gov/files/documents/rcp_s4668_xml.pdf)

### Inferences
- Sec. 112 is drafted as a floor on what associations must permit, paired with immunity for enforcing it. In practice it lets the NCAA reinstate a sit-out year for a second undergraduate transfer, which the 2024 consent decree currently forbids (section 6). The statute lists no path to a third undergraduate transfer other than the four exceptions; how the NCAA would treat one is left to its rules.
- The head-coach-departure exception matters disproportionately at schools with frequent coaching turnover, a common situation in FCS and HBCU football, where successful coaches are hired away: a coaching change would reopen free transfer for the whole roster regardless of prior transfers.
- The graduate-transfer exception keeps the "grad transfer down to an HBCU" pipeline open without limit, while the one-free-transfer rule would slow repeat undergraduate movement in both directions (up to FBS and back down).
- The $50 million student-fee threshold is defined on media-rights revenue, so it would not touch SWAC or MEAC schools, whose athletics budgets depend heavily on institutional support and student fees. There is no subsidy cap, revenue floor or other financing restriction on low-revenue schools in the Senate text.
- Sec. 113's statutory clock permits association-adopted injury exceptions; the NCAA's own June 2026 rule reportedly eliminated injury extensions, so the statute is slightly more permissive than current NCAA policy.

### Gaps
- I did not read Secs. 101, 104, 107-111, 115-117 or Title II in full; descriptions there rely on the cited press and the sponsor fact sheet.
- The CBO estimate and any Congressional Research Service product were not opened. Ropes & Gray's and Morgan Lewis's alerts were blocked.
- Whether HBCU athletic conferences themselves (as opposed to member institutions) can receive Title III grants is not settled by the definition of "eligible entity"; the "HBCU athletic conference" definition is used elsewhere in the title, which I did not trace.

## 6. Legal backdrop for the transfer and eligibility provisions

### Takeaway
The transfer section exists because the NCAA lost the power to make repeat transfers sit out (Ohio v. NCAA, 2023-24, ending in a permanent consent judgment with the Justice Department and states); the eligibility section exists because Pavia v. NCAA (2024-25) opened a wave of more than 80 eligibility suits with inconsistent results, and the NCAA's own June 2026 age-based "five years to play five seasons" rule immediately drew new suits. The PCSA would put both rules in statute, immunize their enforcement from antitrust claims, preempt state law, and confine challenges to federal court.

### Cited Findings
**Transfers**
- Ohio v. NCAA, No. 1:23-cv-100 (N.D. W. Va., Judge John Preston Bailey): a coalition of states won a restraining order and then a preliminary injunction (December 2023) against the NCAA's rule requiring multi-time transfers to sit a year. In April 2024 the NCAA changed its rules so transfers are immediately eligible regardless of the number of prior moves if academically eligible. On May 30, 2024 the Justice Department and ten states plus D.C. filed a proposed consent judgment that permanently bars the NCAA from enforcing the Transfer Eligibility Rule "or any similar rule requiring transfer athletes to meet a year-in-residence requirement", and restores a year of eligibility to certain affected athletes. — [Ohio Attorney General, May 30, 2024](https://ohioattorneygeneral.gov/Media/News-Releases/May-2024/AG-Yost-and-Allies-Score-Major-Win-for-College-Ath); [AP via KVIA, May 30, 2024](https://kvia.com/news/ap-national/2024/05/30/ncaa-states-reach-agreement-to-permanently-allow-multiple-transfer-athletes-to-compete/); [Fox Sports/AP, April 2024](https://www.foxsports.com/articles/cfb/ncaa-allows-transfers-to-be-immediately-eligible-no-matter-how-many-times-theyve-switched-schools) (all: search summary only)
- NCAA's own account in 2026: "lawsuits weakened its transfer policies and caused frequent roster turnover"; the bill would set a one-transfer rule with limited exceptions and limited antitrust protection to enforce it. — [NCAA, Protect College Sports Act page](https://www.ncaa.org/protect-college-sports-act/)
- Transfer windows remain the NCAA's main lever: undergraduates must enter the portal in sport-specific windows to be immediately eligible. On Oct. 7, 2026 the Division I Cabinet approved new windows, including a single 10-day football window (FBS and FCS) beginning the first business day after Jan. 1; in February 2026 it endorsed penalties for programs that circumvent the football window. — NCAA governance updates (search summary only; exact NCAA URLs not confirmed)

**Eligibility**
- Pavia v. NCAA: Vanderbilt quarterback Diego Pavia sued in 2024, arguing junior-college seasons should not count against Division I eligibility, and won a preliminary injunction; the NCAA then issued a blanket one-year waiver for similarly situated former junior-college players. The Sixth Circuit dismissed the NCAA's appeal as moot in a ruling released Sept. 30, 2025 and urged Congress to act; the underlying case is expected to go to trial in early 2027. — [Front Office Sports, July 21, 2026](https://frontofficesports.com/article/new-ncaa-eligibility-policy-hasnt-slowed-lawsuits-its-triggered-more/); [AOL/Tennessean on the appeal dismissal](https://www.aol.com/articles/judge-dismisses-ncaa-appeal-diego-204218153.html) (second source: search summary only)
- Volume: over roughly two years, federal and state courts ruled on more than 80 requests to enjoin NCAA eligibility rules and denied nearly half. — [Bloomberg Law, "NCAA Weighs Five-Year Eligibility Rules as Player Suits Pile Up"](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/in-house-counsel/ncaa-weighs-five-year-eligibility-rules-as-player-suits-pile-up) (search summary only)
- NCAA rule change: proposed April 2026 and approved by the Division I Cabinet on June 23, 2026, an age-based model giving athletes five years to play up to five seasons, with the clock starting at initial full-time enrollment or the academic year after the 19th birthday, whichever is first; it largely ends redshirt and waiver extensions except for religious missions, pregnancy and military service, and no longer extends the clock for injury. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/sports/college-sports/ncaa-panel-approves-new-eligibility-rules-giving-division-athletes-5-y-rcna351531) (search summary only); June 23, 2026 date also cited by [Taft law bulletin](https://www.taftlaw.com/news-events/law-bulletins/congress-moves-closer-to-rewriting-the-rules-of-college-sports-five-things-the-industry-should-know/)
- The new rule did not grandfather players from the high school class of 2022 who had already used four seasons; suits followed within a day: a Hamilton County, Ohio state-court case for 15 basketball players (preliminary injunction granted, including portal access; stay pending appeal denied July 17, 2026), suits in Nashville, Cobb County (Georgia) and California (45 players), and a proposed federal antitrust class action in Colorado filed July 10, 2026. — [Front Office Sports, July 21, 2026](https://frontofficesports.com/article/new-ncaa-eligibility-policy-hasnt-slowed-lawsuits-its-triggered-more/)
- The NCAA Division I board said it had no plans to change the age-based rule. — [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/espn/story/_/id/49435899/no-plans-change-age-based-eligibility) (search summary only)
- The PCSA's Sec. 113 mirrors the NCAA's age-based clock (five calendar years from enrollment or the academic year after the 19th birthday); the NCAA says the statutory period "aligns with the NCAA's age-based rule". — [S. 4668 as engrossed](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4668es/xml/BILLS-119s4668es.xml); [NCAA, Protect College Sports Act page](https://www.ncaa.org/protect-college-sports-act/)
- One law firm notes it is unclear how the NCAA's June 2026 shift from a "Five-Year Rule" to a "Five-Season Rule" interacts with the bill's antitrust analysis. — [Taft law bulletin](https://www.taftlaw.com/news-events/law-bulletins/congress-moves-closer-to-rewriting-the-rules-of-college-sports-five-things-the-industry-should-know/)

### Inferences
- If enacted, Sec. 112 plus Sec. 118 would override the practical effect of the 2024 consent judgment for second undergraduate transfers: a sit-out year could return for 2027-28 at the earliest, depending on NCAA implementing rules. Until enactment, unlimited immediately-eligible transfers (within portal windows) remain the law of the land.
- State-court eligibility suits like the Ohio and Georgia cases are the specific target of the federal-court-only clause and of state-law preemption in Sec. 121.
- Because Sec. 123 preserves pending cases and past-damages claims (except that no one may exceed five total years), enactment would not immediately end the current eligibility docket.
