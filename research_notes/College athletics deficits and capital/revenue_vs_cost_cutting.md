# College athletics: revenue problem vs. cost problem — can budgets be balanced by cost-cutting alone?

Scope note: US NCAA Division I / FBS only. Fiscal years (FY) are as labeled by each source; NCAA and Knight-Newhouse data use the institution's fiscal year (usually July–June; Texas and SEC use Sept–Aug). "Power" = Autonomy/Power Four (plus Pac-12 before 2024) conferences. Research current as of October 22, 2026. Note that Sportico articles redirect to a paywall gateway (tollbit.sportico.com) and could not be fetched directly; Sportico figures below are cited via search snippets or secondary outlets and are flagged as such.

## Key Question 1: How big is Division I / FBS athletics revenue, how fast has it grown, and which lines grew fastest?

### Takeaway
Division I athletics took in ~$20.5B in FY2024 (up 7% YoY) and spent ~$20.8B (up 9% YoY); FBS public programs alone reported $12.8B in both revenue and spending in FY2025. Over FY2015–FY2024 median generated revenue at Power schools grew 56% while median expenses grew 81%; the fastest-growing revenue lines are media rights, conference/CFP distributions and donations, but expense growth has outpaced revenue growth in every subdivision in every multi-year window examined.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The aggregate "revenue problem vs. cost problem" framing is subdivision-dependent: Power schools are ~89% self-sufficient and their revenue grew ~56% in a decade, yet their median deficit still widened because expenses grew 81%; Group of Five and below are structurally dependent on allocated revenue (institutional support is the single largest "revenue" line for all non-Power groups).
- The gap between revenue growth (+56–60% nominal) and inflation (+31%) shows revenue has grown in real terms; the problem at the top is that spending grew faster than even that.

## Key Question 2: Media rights deals, per-school conference distributions over time, and whether the next cycle will grow or plateau

### Takeaway
Big Ten and SEC per-school distributions roughly doubled in six years (Big Ten ~$55.6M in FY2019 → $76.1M announced / $79.7M full-share in FY2025; SEC ~$45.3M in FY2019 → $72.4M in FY2025), driven by new ESPN/Fox/CBS/NBC deals and a tripled CFP contract ($1.3B/yr from 2026-27). But the Big Ten deal runs only through 2029-30, cable's decline is shrinking the pool, and analysts expect "more modest" gains next cycle absent structural change.

## Key Question 3: How concentrated is the revenue (and its growth) in the Big Ten/SEC vs. the rest?

### Takeaway
The 69 Power schools generated $10B of the $13.1B in D-I generated revenue in FY2023-24 (more than three times the other 283 schools combined), and the Big Two's new media and CFP deals widen the gap further: the average Power-conference TV distribution was ~$47.3M per school in FY2023 vs. ~$4M in lower FBS conferences, and the Big Ten/SEC are expected to take ~60% of the $1.3B CFP pool.

### Cited Findings
- "While FBS autonomy schools accounted for about 55% of the Division I total revenue ($20.5 billion), they accounted for almost 77% of the total generated revenue." — [NCAA Research, Dec. 2025](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2025D1RES_DI-RevExpReport_Final.pdf)
- "Power colleges generated $10 billion in revenue—more than three times the combined total of all other DI colleges ($3 billion)"; "The majority of the Power colleges (the middle 60 percent) generated $98.2 million to $191.5 million in revenue, which is more than any college generated" in the other subdivisions. Median Power spending ~$167M was 3–7x the other subdivisions' medians (~$49M, $25M, $23M); "The lowest spending Power college spent nearly $90 million." — [GAO-26-108640, July 2026](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108640.pdf)
- Knight Commission (FY2023 data): median total revenue of institutions in the Defendant (Power) conferences is $145M, "approximately $100 million greater than the median institution in the other FBS conferences [$42M] and approximately $125 million greater than the median institutions" in FCS ($19M) and non-football D-I ($18M). — [Knight Commission Brief on House v. NCAA, Feb. 12, 2025](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/KnightCommissionBrief_HousevNCAA_182025.pdf)
- Conference distribution gap (Group of Five vs. Power 5 average per school): $6.34M (2002) → $11.31M (2010) → $37.47M (2020) → $43.35M (FY2023). Troy was highest at 82%." — [InvestigateTV, Aug. 24, 2026](https://www.investigatetv.com/2026/08/24/billion-dollar-blitz-college-sports-big-business-is-bankrolled-by-taxpayers-students/)
- One academic analysis: subsidies average "56 percent at the Group of Five level and 69 percent at the FCS level"; an average G5 program (~$47.5M revenue vs. ~$48.7M expenses, −$1.18M) would move to roughly −$11.2M with $10M of revenue sharing. — [PMC, "From NIL to real estate: strategic revenue adaptation in the post-house era"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12852404/)

### Inferences
- Nearly all incremental media/CFP dollars since FY2024 flow to ~34 Big Ten/SEC schools; for the other ~95 FBS schools, conference distributions have been flat to modestly up, so the House cost layer (Q4) lands on budgets whose revenue is not growing.

## Key Question 4: What new cost layer did the House v. NCAA settlement create, and how are schools funding it?

### Takeaway
House (approved June 6, 2025) adds up to $20.5M per school in direct athlete pay in 2025-26 (cap ~$21.3M–$21.58M in 2026-27, +4%/yr, re-benchmarked every three years), plus scholarship expansion under roster limits, plus $2.78B in back damages funded 60% through reduced NCAA distributions over 10 years. Schools are covering it mainly with new student fees, ticket surcharges, internal loans, institutional transfers, state money, reserves and selective cuts rather than with new external revenue.

### Cited Findings

**Settlement terms**
- Back damages: "approximately $2.78 billion, to be paid over 10 years. Of this amount, NCAA reserves and insurance is expected to cover $1.1 billion and the remaining $1.6 billion will be withheld from future distributions to DI members"; "40 percent of the funds coming from the institutions in the five Defendant Conferences and 60 percent being assessed on Non-Defendant Conferences"; "more than 95 percent of the damages will be paid to" football and men's/women's basketball athletes at Defendant Conference schools. Annual payment ~$280M. — [Knight Commission Brief on House v. NCAA Settlement, Feb. 12, 2025](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/KnightCommissionBrief_HousevNCAA_182025.pdf)
- NCAA reportedly planned to withhold from six distribution funds (basketball performance fund, grants-in-aid, academic enhancement, sports sponsorship, conference grants, academic performance), leaving the equal conference fund, student-athlete opportunity fund and special assistance fund untouched. — [Pete Thamel, ESPN, May 17, 2024](https://africa.espn.com/college-sports/story/_/id/40167617/ncaa-settlement-plan-house-v-ncaa-case-irks-non-power-5-schools)
- Per-conference shares reported as 24% Power Five, 10% Group of Five, 14% FCS, 12% non-football D-I (of the member-withheld portion). — [Fisher Phillips, June 2025](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/plan-after-final-approval-granted-in-house-v-ncaa-authorizing-pay-for-student-athletes)
- Cap formula: "The cap for new payments to athletes is based on a formula – 22 percent of the average of specific revenue categories for the institutions in the Defendant Conferences... The institutional cap is recalculated every three years and increases by 4% annually." Year 1 (2025-26) cap estimated at $20.5M. — [Knight Commission Brief, Feb. 2025](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/KnightCommissionBrief_HousevNCAA_182025.pdf)
- 2026-27 cap: the College Sports Commission lists approximately $21.58M per school for 2026-27 with another 4% expected for 2027-28 — [College Sports Commission, "Revenue Sharing"](https://www.collegesportscommission.org/revenue-sharing/); other sources (NC State budget, law-firm summaries) use ~$21.3M. — [WRAL, Feb. 19, 2026](https://www.wral.com/sports/ncstate-financial-outlook-boo-corrigan-budget-presentation-february-2026/); [Horgan Law](https://horganlawfirm.com/nil-nebraska-2026-revenue-sharing-house-settlement-update/). Conflict noted: $21.3M vs. $21.58M.
- Participation: "For that year [2025-26], 310 of 361 DI colleges participated in the revenue sharing arrangement under this settlement." — [GAO-26-108640, July 2026](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108640.pdf)

## Key Question 5: Cost structure — what is fixed vs. variable, and how large is athletics debt?

### Takeaway
At a typical Power department, roughly 55–60% of spending is contractually or structurally committed in the short run: coaching compensation (~19%), facilities/debt service/equipment (~22% at FBS publics; ~$94M at Texas), scholarships (~11–14%) and multi-year staff contracts; FBS public athletics debt totals $11.4B (FY2025), with a Power Four median of $120.3M and outliers at Penn State ($534.7M) and Florida State ($437M).

### Cited Findings

**Budget composition**
- FBS public operating expenses FY2025: facilities/debt service/equipment 22%; coaches 19%; non-coaching staff 16%; scholarships 14%; game expenses and travel 12%; other 10%; recruiting 2%; guarantees 2%; medical 1%; coaching severance 1%; institutional NIL revenue share 0% (partial year). — [Knight Commission KNAD FY2025 one-pager](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/KNAD_database_information_FY2025.pdf)
- NCAA FY2024: Power schools spent >$1.2B on athletics aid (~11% of spending); "coach compensation was the largest spending category, accounting for almost 19% of total expenses" for both autonomy and nonautonomy FBS; "coach compensation as a proportion of total expenses has been relatively consistent in the range of 18%-20%... total compensation as a proportion of total expenses... ranged from 32%-36%." — [NCAA Research, Dec. 2025](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2025D1RES_DI-RevExpReport_Final.pdf)
- GAO FY2023-24 Power medians: coaching compensation $30.8M (14 schools >$38M; lowest ~$14M); support staff $26.9M; scholarships $17.5M; team and game expenses $10.3M; recruiting $3.1M (up 115% from $1.4M in 2014-15). Facilities (leases, rent, debt service) were 6–7% of spending at Power and Non-Power schools. Power schools' largest decade increase was in coaching; Non-Power's was in support staff. — [GAO-26-108640, July 2026](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108640.pdf)
- Football share: "Football accounts for 30% of athletic spending at public FBS schools. At Colorado State, football consumes half the athletic budget. Clemson, Florida State and Washington are among six schools where it exceeds 40%." Head football coach salaries rose 40% in five years; football staff salaries rose 33%; 169 assistants/GMs earn >$108M this year; at least 42 assistants earn >$1M; Kirby Smart $13.6M, Ryan Day $12.1M, Dan Lanning $11.6M, with eight others >$10M. — [InvestigateTV, Aug. 24, 2026](https://www.investigatetv.com/2026/08/24/billion-dollar-blitz-college-sports-big-business-is-bankrolled-by-taxpayers-students/)
- Ohio State spent $54M on coaching salaries/benefits/bonuses and >$33M on facility debt service and leases; Arizona's facility debt (~$25M) roughly matched its coaching or support-staff spend; "over a dozen other P5 schools spent more than $10 million on it." — [Matt Brown, Extra Points, Aug. 25, 2025](https://www.extrapointsmb.com/p/here-s-the-real-reason-why-athletic-department-budgets-keep-going-up-24e0bbd86498ef5b)
- Texas FY2025: facility debt service and rental fees ~$94M (largest line), coaching $53M, support staff $50.3M, of $375.9M total. — [SwimSwam](https://swimswam.com/2025-ncaa-budgets-texas-reports-record-375-9-million-in-spending-lsu-football-booms/)
- Knight/CLA "crossover point": by FY2032, 25 of 54 public Autonomy 5 schools (46%) are projected to pay their 11 countable football coaches more ($1.363B) than they spend on scholarships and medical for all athletes ($1.372B); 9 of 51 had crossed in FY2022. Severance ("dead money"): $98M in FY2022, projected >$175M/yr (conservative) to $481M/yr (trend method) by FY2032. — [Knight Commission, Sept. 6, 2023](https://www.knightcommission.org/2023/09/financial-projections-through-2032-for-division-i-fbs-programs/)

**Fixed/sticky obligations**
- Brown: "a multi-year coaching contract is hard to unwind, since it is difficult to claw back money on high level coaching salaries"; debt from past construction "can't be undone, and costs like insurance, travel, and scholarships are hard to cut quickly, so spending stays high even if revenue growth slows." — [Extra Points, Aug. 25, 2025](https://www.extrapointsmb.com/p/here-s-the-real-reason-why-athletic-department-budgets-keep-going-up-24e0bbd86498ef5b)
- Texas CFO Novak on debt service: "it'll continue to look like this for the next five or six years while we pay off all those big projects." — [Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/texas-athletic-department-reports-loss-221817768.html)
- Kansas CFO Pat Kaufman: "We knew at the time we were going to run a couple years of deficits while we broke the stadium in"; "This is intentional to land in 2028." — [KU Sports, Oct. 1, 2025](https://www2.kusports.com/sports/college/general/2025/oct/01/ku-athletics-projects-deficit-of-14-9-million-for-fiscal-year-2026/)
- Cal: borrowed ~$445M (94% of cost) for the 2011 stadium project; debt-service payments of $18M "will escalate to $37 million in 2039... Anticipated higher ticket sales have not materialized... The payment schedule, incredibly, runs through the year 2112." Cal's outstanding athletics debt was $432M as of FY2024. — [Cantwell office, Sept. 2026](https://www.cantwell.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/spiraling_college_sports_costs_snapshot2.pdf); [Saturday Down South](https://www.saturdaydownsouth.com/news/college-football/acc-program-leads-nation-in-athletic-debt-for-2025-financial-year/)

**Athletics debt**

### Inferences
- Combining Knight-Newhouse FY2025 shares, the lines that are hardest to cut within one or two budget cycles (coaching contracts 19% + severance 1%, debt service/facilities ~22%, scholarships 14%, guarantees 2%) are ~58% of FBS public spending; the plausibly variable remainder (non-coaching staff 16%, game/travel 12%, other 10%, recruiting 2%, medical 1%) is ~41%, and much of that is also contractual (multi-year staff deals, scheduled games, conference travel footprints that grew with realignment).
- Because scholarships are expanding under House roster limits and revenue sharing is a new quasi-fixed line (schools treat the cap as the competitive floor), the fixed share is rising, not falling.

### Gaps
- No current (FY2024/FY2025) FBS-wide annual debt-service figure (as opposed to outstanding debt) was found; the $578M (Power 5, 2020) and $165M (G5, 2021) Knight figures are the latest located.
- No consultant (Huron, Deloitte, CSA) study quantifying "fixed vs. variable" shares was found; the fixed/variable split above is an inference from category data.

## Key Question 6: What has happened when schools tried to cut, and what are the limits (Title IX, NCAA minimums, buyouts, politics)?

### Takeaway
Cost-cutting to date has been concentrated in the smallest lines (Olympic sports at ~$300K–$800K each, 5–10% staff reductions saving ~$3M), while the largest discretionary line — football coaching — hit a record $228M in buyouts in 2025 alone. Title IX, the 16-sport FBS minimum, donor/political backlash (Stanford 2020–21) and multi-year contracts make deep cuts slow and legally risky, and none of the documented cuts approach the size of the House cost layer.

## Key Question 7: Revenue problem or cost problem — what do economists, consultants and the Knight Commission say about how much could realistically be cut?

### Takeaway
The dominant expert view (Zimbalist, Knight Commission/CLA, Matt Brown) is that top-tier college sports has an incentive/cost-discipline problem — new revenue is reliably spent on coaches, staff and facilities because no one is rewarded for restraint — while the structural view (GAO, Cantwell, ADs like Corrigan and Dickey) stresses that revenue growth is now concentrated in ~34 schools and slowing elsewhere just as athlete pay becomes a quasi-fixed, escalating line. No source found quantifies a realistic cost-cutting ceiling; the evidence suggests departments outside the Big Two cannot close $15–27M gaps through cuts alone without breaching Title IX, NCAA minimums or coaching contracts.

### Cited Findings

**"It's a cost/incentive problem" camp**
- Andrew Zimbalist (Smith College): "Unlike the rest of the U.S. economy, where there's a lot of cost discipline that comes from the marketplace, there is no such discipline in college sports"; "The reason is that college sports administrations in different schools don't have stockholders"; "Whenever a new pile of money falls into the lap of an athletic director, it is deployed to promote more victories"; "If there are 135 other schools out there doing basically the same thing, then it becomes part of the culture." — [InvestigateTV, Aug. 24, 2026](https://www.investigatetv.com/2026/08/24/billion-dollar-blitz-college-sports-big-business-is-bankrolled-by-taxpayers-students/)
- Matt Brown: revenue sharing "is part of the problem, but the 'actual major issue' is incentives and governance"; "But nobody gets promoted because of their financial restraint"; AD bonus structures (Marshall's $65K pool; Arizona's $50K bowl bonus) reward wins and revenue, not spending control; "a leadership and an incentive problem." Boise State AD Jeramiah Dickey: "We don't have unity across our industry." — [Extra Points, Aug. 25, 2025](https://www.extrapointsmb.com/p/here-s-the-real-reason-why-athletic-department-budgets-keep-going-up-24e0bbd86498ef5b)
- Knight Commission co-chair Len Elmore: CLA's projections "should shift the attention of Division I FBS leaders from the pursuit of even more revenue"; Arne Duncan: "business-as-usual athletics spending with billions in new revenue" is incongruent with the mission; Eric Barron: "it is much easier to revamp spending priorities with new revenue" than to reallocate existing spending. — [Knight Commission, Sept. 6, 2023](https://www.knightcommission.org/2023/09/financial-projections-through-2032-for-division-i-fbs-programs/)
- Knight Commission 2020: FBS public institutions' scholarship spending "was less than a quarter of what these institutions spent cumulatively on facilities" ($1.75B on facilities and equipment in 2017). — [Knight Commission, Aug. 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/2020/08/the-coming-covid-19-transformation-of-college-sports/)
- NCAA's own dashboard: median FBS autonomy school was 89% self-sufficient in 2024, "the lowest except for 2021 with COVID-19 impact"; athletics-to-institutional expense ratio stable at 6–7%; "the median expense growth gap between athletics spending and institutional spending is -0.2% for autonomy schools and 2.5% for nonautonomy FBS schools." — [NCAA Research, Dec. 2025](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2025D1RES_DI-RevExpReport_Final.pdf)
- Dan Rascher and Andy Schwarz (2019): college sports is the most extreme example of perverse incentives from revenue sharing and salary caps because revenue sharing shares revenues but not costs. — [Rascher & Schwarz via search; see also Rascher 2011 paper](https://journals.ku.edu/jis/article/download/10022/21975/93075) (secondary; exact article not fetched)

**"Revenue is now structurally short for most" camp**
- GAO: "94 percent of all DI college athletics programs (330 of 352)" spent more than they generated; "This gap widened from a decade earlier as spending increased more than generated revenue in all DI subdivisions"; revenue-sharing cap "is scheduled to increase in subsequent years." — [GAO-26-108640, July 2026](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108640.pdf)
- Academic analysis: "unless athletic department revenues increase enough to offset the 22% revenue share allocation, athletic departments are likely to face long-term structural deficits." — [PMC, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12852404/)
- NC State AD Boo Corrigan: "To remain competitive, we need to be in this world"; "Everyone is pulling every level you can"; "We got to get better. We got to keep investing." — [WRAL, Feb. 19, 2026](https://www.wral.com/sports/ncstate-financial-outlook-boo-corrigan-budget-presentation-february-2026/)
- UNC reported a $15M athletics deficit for FY2025. — [WRAL, Feb. 19, 2026](https://www.wral.com/sports/ncstate-financial-outlook-boo-corrigan-budget-presentation-february-2026/)
- Washington AD Pat Chun on the Big Ten half-share gap: "They have Phil Knight. We don't." — [Press Democrat, May 5, 2026](https://www.pressdemocrat.com/2026/05/05/big-ten-revenue-payouts-media-deal-west-coast-schools/)
- UW-Madison AD Chris McIntosh to Wisconsin lawmakers: without $14.6M in state support "we will be forced to reconcile our revenues with our liabilities … through a series of painful reductions." — [Cantwell office, Sept. 2026](https://www.cantwell.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/spiraling_college_sports_costs_snapshot2.pdf)

**Donor/ticket/fee fatigue and reliance signals**
- NACUBO: endowments funded 15.2% of university operating budgets in FY2025 (vs. 10.9% in FY2023); new gifts declined 9.2% from FY2024; meanwhile mega-gifts to athletics include $401M to Michigan State, $300M to Kansas, $100M each to Creighton and Illinois. — [Cantwell office, Sept. 2026](https://www.cantwell.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/spiraling_college_sports_costs_snapshot2.pdf)

- Tennessee's talent fee: the department "hoped that linking the increase to athlete pay would blunt backlash, but many social media posts complaining about the price hike still gained traction." — [CBS Sports, Sept. 2024](https://www.cbssports.com/college-football/news/tennessee-set-to-add-10-talent-fee-to-tickets-to-fund-plan-for-revenue-sharing-with-players)
- NC State explicitly declined "an across-the-board 18% performance fee to tickets." — [WRAL, Feb. 19, 2026](https://www.wral.com/sports/ncstate-financial-outlook-boo-corrigan-budget-presentation-february-2026/)
- Student-fee reliance rose 13% in five years at public FBS schools; Old Dominion undergraduates each paid ~$1,900 in athletic fees (>$33M total; ~$7,600 over four years); Indiana's university funding to athletics rose tenfold to nearly $25M; Florida more than tripled its reliance on students. — [InvestigateTV, Aug. 24, 2026](https://www.investigatetv.com/2026/08/24/billion-dollar-blitz-college-sports-big-business-is-bankrolled-by-taxpayers-students/)
- Cal: "Anticipated higher ticket sales have not materialized" on the stadium debt; "The university itself has now taken over part of the burden of the debt payments." — [Cantwell office, Sept. 2026](https://www.cantwell.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/spiraling_college_sports_costs_snapshot2.pdf)

### Inferences
- Both camps are consistent with the data: Big Ten/SEC schools have a cost-discipline problem (revenue up ~$20M per school in FY2025, buyouts up to $54M, roster budgets at $40M); everyone else has a revenue problem layered on top of inherited fixed costs (debt service, contracts) and a new quasi-mandatory athlete-pay line indexed at +4%/yr.
- A "pure cost-cutting" path to balance would require touching coaching compensation and facility commitments — the lines that are contractually locked and competitively protected — because the lines that are actually being cut (Olympic sports, support staff) are too small and are constrained by Title IX and the 16-sport rule.

### Gaps
- No quantified estimate from any economist or consultancy (Huron, Deloitte, Navigate, Elevate, Collegiate Sports Associates, Karen Weaver, Jeff Smith) of how much a typical department could realistically cut was found; searches returned only qualitative commentary.
- No systematic evidence on season-ticket renewal or donor-count declines after talent fees/price increases (Tennessee's post-2025 renewal data not found); Utah reported record donor counts in FY2025, cutting against a "donor fatigue" narrative.
- Daniel Rascher's and Karen Weaver's direct statements on post-House cost-cutting were not located.
