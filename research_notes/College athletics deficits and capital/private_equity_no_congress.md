## Key Question 1: Private-capital deals and proposals (proposed or completed through October 2026), amounts, structures, and outcomes

### Takeaway
Through October 2026 only two private-capital transactions have actually closed in major college sports: the Big 12's five-year, non-equity "Collegiate Athletic Solutions" (RedBird/Weatherford) deal (approved late April 2026; $12.5M to the league office plus an optional $30M-per-school credit line that, as of July 2026, no school had drawn and 13 of 16 had publicly declined) and the University of Utah's school-level equity spin-off with Otro Capital (board-authorized Dec. 9, 2025; closed June 2026; terms undisclosed, widely reported as "up to $500M" in projected revenue). The marquee conference-wide equity deal, the Big Ten's $2.4B / 10% "Big Ten Enterprises" transaction with UC Investments, was paused by the investor on Nov. 17, 2025 after Michigan and USC objected, and no revival had been reported by October 2026; the ACC, SEC and American have explored but not transacted; earlier Big 12/CVC (2024), Florida State/Sixth Street (2023–24), and the Smash Capital "Project Rudy" super-league pitch (2024–) never closed.

### Cited Findings

**Big Ten / UC Investments / "Big Ten Enterprises"**
- Framework (fall 2025): a new entity, Big Ten Enterprises, would hold all leaguewide media rights and sponsorship contracts; it would be divided into 20 equity shares (18 schools, the conference office, and one outside investor); UC Investments would receive a 10% stake with typical minority-investor rights but no direct control, held for at least 15 years; infusion "more than $2 billion"; tiered initial payments with the lowest in the nine-figure range and larger departments above $150M; implementation "will require unanimous league support" — [ESPN, Pete Thamel, "Sources: Big Ten discussing $2 billion private capital deal"](https://www.espn.com/college-sports/story/_/id/46452188/sources-big-ten-discussing-2-billion-private-capital-deal); [ESPN, "Big Ten closes in on $2B capital agreement vote"](https://www.espn.com/college-sports/story/_/id/46553869/big-ten-closes-2-billion-capital-agreement-vote-per-sources)
- UC Investments' offer let schools "buy down" the investor's ownership stake over time — [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/uc-investments-big-10-offer-allows-teams-to-buy-down-ownership-stakes)
- Deal size $2.4B for a 10% stake; schools would sign a grant-of-rights binding them to the conference until 2046; UC Investments began talks in July 2025; on Nov. 17, 2025 UC Investments said it would not sign without Michigan and USC and "requires some additional time to complete our due diligence"; it called the unity of the 18 members "key to the success of Big Ten Enterprises"; UC Investments investment director Dianne Klein said the fund is "nothing like private equity" and "not private capital at all"; Michigan regent Jordan Acker: "I think it's the right decision"; Penn State trustee Jay Paterno said the Big Ten's presentation to him was "one slide that had five lines on it"; trustees from Maryland, USC, Michigan, Penn State and Minnesota said they were largely kept in the dark and told presidents could sign without board approval — [Front Office Sports, Amanda Christovich, Nov. 17, 2025](https://frontofficesports.com/uc-investments-says-big-ten-deal-is-off-until-schools-can-agree/)
- ESPN's report on the pause — [ESPN, "Opposition from Michigan, USC pauses $2.4B Big Ten deal"](https://www.espn.com/college-sports/story/_/id/47003108/opposition-michigan-usc-pauses-24b-big-ten-deal); earlier, [ESPN, "Sources: USC, Michigan question proposed Big Ten private capital deal"](https://www.espn.com/college-sports/story/_/id/46597733/sources-usc-michigan-question-proposed-big-ten-private-capital-deal)
- Michigan regents' position (Jan. 26, 2026): Board chair Mark Bernstein read a statement at the November 2025 meeting: "We remain opposed to this deal." Regent Sarah Hubbard (R) said the grant of rights through 2046 is too long and could limit the university's ability to innovate; "There may be a way where it works for Michigan, but we have not yet made any decisions on that." Payments would be about $150M to each of the 18 schools with remaining capital distributed by earning potential; UC Investments would receive 10% of revenue from media rights and sponsorships for 15 years; Michigan's athletic budget deficit was nearly $27M; there is no set timeline; approval from all 18 schools is required per UC Investments' CIO; individual schools may pursue their own private-equity deals — [The Michigan Daily, Ava Cobb, Jan. 26, 2026](https://www.michigandaily.com/news/administration/umich-regents-oppose-2-4-billion-big-ten-private-equity-deal/)

### Inferences
- The market has converged on non-equity structures (conference-level revenue partnerships, school credit lines, vendor-layer PE such as Learfield) because governance vetoes, long grant-of-rights demands and public-university politics have blocked conference equity sales; the only closed equity deal (Utah) is at a single school that controls its own board.
- Even where capital is available (Big 12's $480M of undrawn credit; Elevate's $500M), departments are declining it: the binding constraint appears to be willingness to take on double-digit-rate debt against future distributions, not access to capital.

## Key Question 2: What are the actual rules/obstacles? What would "private equity is not allowed" mean in practice?

### Takeaway
There is no NCAA bylaw, federal statute or state law located that flatly bans private capital in college athletics; the operative constraints are (1) conference governance (UC Investments required unanimity of 18 Big Ten members; two boards of regents/trustees vetoed it), (2) fiduciary and political opposition from public-university boards and members of Congress, (3) the long grant-of-rights (to 2046) investors demand, (4) tax-exempt-bond "private business use" limits and state restrictions on encumbering public assets, and (5) the Senate-passed Protect College Sports Act's new Sports Broadcasting Act provisions, which would make it unlawful under the antitrust laws for "any entity" to merge or acquire the media rights of power-conference schools to create a new conference (i.e., a Project Rudy-style PE super league) while remaining silent on minority stakes in a conference's commercial entity.

### Inferences
- "If private equity is not allowed" most plausibly means, in practice: (a) a conference's members fail to reach the unanimity or supermajority its investor requires (the Big Ten case), (b) public boards of regents refuse to pledge media rights or extend grants of rights, (c) Congress enacts the PCSA's Title II consolidation ban (killing PE-financed super leagues) and/or adds explicit restrictions, or (d) state legislatures restrict encumbrance of public assets. None of the located sources shows an NCAA bylaw or federal law that would bar a minority investment in a conference-owned commercial entity.
- The PCSA Title II text targets consolidation of media rights by "any entity," which would reach a Smash Capital-style buyer, but appears not to reach a UC Investments-style minority stake in an existing conference's commercial subsidiary; this distinction is an inference from the Brownstein summary, not from a located legal analysis.

## Key Question 3: Federal legislation status (as of Oct. 22, 2026) and what happens without a bill

### Takeaway
The House's SCORE Act (H.R. 4312) has been pulled from the floor twice (December 2025 and May 19, 2026) amid Congressional Black Caucus and some Republican opposition and never received a vote; the Senate instead passed the Cruz–Cantwell Protect College Sports Act (S. 4668) 77–22 on Sept. 28, 2026 with NCAA, SEC, Big Ten and White House support, and it now awaits the House, which is in recess until after the Nov. 3 midterms. **PCSA — key provisions as passed (Brownstein section-by-section, Oct. 1, 2026; The Athletic explainer, Sept. 29, 2026)**
- Antitrust limitation of liability (§118): not unlawful under antitrust laws for an association, conference or institution to enforce/comply with the prohibited-compensation rules (§114), revenue cap and retention fund (§115), eligibility (§113), NIL reporting (§101), recruiting/tampering (§117), transfers (§112), championship selection, agent registry (§103) and mid-season coaching rules (§110), and to fine, restrict competition or restrict eligibility for violations; exemptions are conditioned on the association implementing all nine categories and four penalty regimes; an entity "under its control pursuant to the House settlement" (i.e., the College Sports Commission) may enforce §114 after notice-and-comment to House defendants — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf)
- Revenue cap: defined by reference to the House settlement "Benefits Pool Limit"; §114 adds a "retention fund" exception of up to $22.5M per year above the cap for athletes who have spent one full season at the school, plus $5M for non-revenue (women's/Olympic) programs, available only to schools meeting academic benchmarks and only for nine years after enactment; §115: if the House settlement expires/terminates and Congress does not pass a joint resolution, the cap and retention fund lapse; upon continuation the cap rises 4% per year for the first two years — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf); AP: the Senate bill "would raise the cap to $48.8 million," with the extra described as a "retention pool" intended to pull third-party NIL money back under the cap — [AP via La Nación, Oct. 2, 2026](https://www.lanacion.com.ar/usa/in-first-year-of-nil-payouts-68-schools-hit-or-came-very-close-to-205m-revenue-sharing-limit-nid01102026/); The Athletic: total "nearly $50 million a year" — [SLTrib/The Athletic, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/)
- Employment: §122 — "This title is neutral on, and does nothing to alter, employee or non-employee status for student athletes." §116 creates a 24-member Congressional Commission on the Future of College Athletics to study compensation structures "including collective bargaining implications and employment status," with a preliminary report in 30 months — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf)
- Preemption (§121): field preemption of state laws on NIL compensation, transfers and eligibility; conflict preemption across Title I; pending cases and damages claims for pre-enactment conduct preserved (§123) — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf)
- Women's and Olympic sports (§125): no reduction in minimum sport-sponsorship numbers; institutions with ≥$80M athletics revenue must maintain at least the 2024–25 level of grant-in-aid opportunities and roster spots for non-revenue sports (nine-year sunset), with a revenue-decline waiver requiring a ≥15% decline and a prior proportional cut to revenue-sport coaching compensation; $50M–$80M schools face a similar four-year requirement; §127 Title IX savings clause; §126 requires "adequate representation" of mid-sized conferences (<$500M revenue) on governing boards — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf)
- Medical: §106 requires five years of post-eligibility medical coverage and an association fund of $60M–$100M to help schools under $20M in revenue — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf)
- Sports Broadcasting Act (Title II): antitrust protection for a "covered entity" pooling college telecast rights only if it initially includes ≥75% of FBS institutions and offers membership to every D-I conference and school; unanimous votes required for revenue distribution and which media rights are contributed; conferences >$700M revenue capped at 20 members and barred from mergers; the Big Ten, SEC and Notre Dame together hold about 25.4% of FBS, enough to block pooling; pooling cannot replace an active contract including the CFP's — [BHFS](https://www.bhfs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Section-by-Section-Review_Protect-College-Sports-Act-of-2026.pdf); [SLTrib/The Athletic, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/)
- Other: one free undergraduate transfer; five years of eligibility; agent fees capped at 5%; "Lane Kiffin Rule" on mid-season coaching moves; nonbinding Jan. 8 postseason end date; HBCU media program — [SLTrib/The Athletic, Sept. 29, 2026](https://www.sltrib.com/sports/2026/09/29/whats-protect-college-sports-act/)

## Key Question 4: If no PE and no Congress — what financing/absorption paths remain, and evidence each is being used

### Takeaway
The default path is already in use: deficits are being covered by university general funds and mandatory student fees (institutional/government support at 53 public Power Four schools rose by $519.9M from 2015 to 2025; general-fund contributions at power-conference schools nearly tripled from $445M to $1.2B), by state tax dollars and tax breaks (Wisconsin $14.6M/yr, Connecticut credit, New Jersey $5M, Florida $22.5M auxiliary transfers), by record debt (median Power Four athletics debt $120.3M; Penn State $534.7M; FSU $437M), by donors and endowment draws, and by cutting rosters and non-revenue programs (32 to 40+ D-I Olympic programs cut in 2024–25; "more than 100" women's/Olympic programs since 2023 per Cantwell), with only 68 of 319 opted-in schools spending to the $20.5M cap in year one.

## Key Question 5: Expert and stakeholder predictions about tiering, at-risk schools and a wave of cuts

### Takeaway
Stakeholders across the spectrum now openly describe a two-tier future: Sen. Cruz says without legislation the system could "kill off everyone but the SEC and Big Ten," Notre Dame AD Pete Bevacqua sees a 24–30 school super league as possible if legislation fails, NC State AD Boo Corrigan foresees a split into paid revenue sports and "super clubs," and Senate data show only 68 schools (the Power Four footprint) spending to the cap while 94% of D-I programs run deficits; the most exposed are non-power publics with high fee dependence (Virginia schools, USF, UMass, Rutgers) and the Olympic-sport programs that bear cuts first.

### Cited Findings
- Cruz (June 3, 2026 hearing): failure to pass the bill could "kill off everyone but the SEC and Big Ten"; a super league would be "devastating for the rest of the country"; Cantwell compared the situation to the Pac-12's breakup; Bevacqua: a small group of schools could eventually dominate, with a 24–30 school super league possible if legislation fails; Gee argued pooled rights could grow the pie; Saban urged a central college sports "commissioner" — [Yahoo Sports, Dellenger, June 4, 2026](https://sports.yahoo.com/college-football/article/sec-big-ten-vs-everybody-protect-college-sports-bill-politicians-rail-against-power-2-leagues-in-pivotal-hearing-021248938.html)

### Inferences
- Combining the CSC filing (68 at the cap), GAO (49 of 69 power schools in deficit) and Cantwell (14 of 53 Power Four self-sufficient) implies that even the "haves" tier is largely subsidy-dependent; the tiering is between schools whose campuses/donors will absorb $20M+ deficits and those (fee-dependent non-power publics) that cannot.
- If the PCSA fails in the House, §125's floor on non-revenue roster spots at ≥$80M schools disappears, leaving sport cuts as the main cost lever at the top tier, while at the bottom tier the lever is not opting in or reclassifying (Saint Francis model).
