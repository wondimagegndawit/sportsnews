# Pre-NIL (pre-July 2021) operating deficits in US college athletic departments and their structural causes

Scope note on definitions (important for reconciling sources):
- **NCAA Research ("Revenues and Expenses" / "Trends in Division I Athletics Finances")**: covers all ~350 D-I members (public and private) via the NCAA Membership Financial Reporting System. "Generated revenues" = ticket sales, NCAA/conference distributions, media rights, donor contributions, guarantees, sponsorships/royalties, camps, endowment income. "Allocated revenues" = student activity fees, direct government support, direct institutional support, indirect institutional support. "Net generated revenue" = generated revenue minus total expenses; a school is "self-sufficient" only if generated revenue ≥ total expenses. Fiscal years run July 1–June 30 (FY2019 = 2018-19). Data since FY2005 only (methodology changed in 2004-05; earlier data "not comparable"). — [NCAA, 15-Year Trends in Division I Athletics Finances (2020 report, FY2005-FY2019)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- **Knight Commission CAFI / Knight-Newhouse database**: public D-I schools only (about 220+ institutions; 107-109 public FBS schools), built from the same NCAA financial reports obtained via public records, back to 2005; adds debt and institution-wide academic spending. — [Knight Commission, March 2019 database launch](https://www.knightcommission.org/2019/03/knight-commission-unveils-new-college-sports-financial-database/)
- **USA Today / Syracuse Newhouse "NCAA Finances"**: public D-I schools only (e.g., 228 in 2012), from the same NCAA reports; "subsidy" = student fees + direct/indirect institutional support + state/government support. — [USA Today analysis republished by Knight Commission, June 6, 2013](https://www.knightcommission.org/2013/06/most-ncaa-division-i-athletic-departments-take-subsidies/)
- **Chronicle of Higher Education / HuffPost "Sports at Any Cost" (Nov 2015)**: 201 public D-I schools, FY2010-2014, inflation-adjusted; "subsidies" = student fees, school-allocated funds, government support. — [HuffPost/Chronicle reporters' note, Nov. 15, 2015](https://projects.huffingtonpost.com/projects/ncaa/reporters-note)
- **Washington Post "Running Up the Bills" (Nov–Dec 2015)**: 48 of the 53 public Power Five schools, FY2004 vs FY2014, inflation-adjusted. — [Poynter, Jan. 21, 2016](https://www.poynter.org/?p=392532)
Because samples differ (all D-I vs. public-only; FBS vs. all D-I) counts of "self-sufficient" programs differ across sources (e.g., NCAA: 25 of ~130 FBS in FY2019; USA Today: 23 of 228 public D-I in FY2012; Chronicle/HuffPost: 21 of 201 public D-I over FY2010-14).

## Key Question 1: How many FBS / D-I programs generated more revenue than expenses before 2021, and what was the median FBS deficit?

### Takeaway
In every year from FY2005 through FY2020, only 16–29 of the ~120–130 FBS schools had generated revenue exceeding expenses; the median FBS program ran a net generated deficit that grew from roughly $7 million (FY2006) to $18.8 million (FY2019) and $19.3 million (FY2020). No FCS or non-football D-I school was self-sufficient in any year of the period.

### Cited Findings
- NCAA table "Division I FBS Average Positive Generated Net Revenue (PNR) for Those Schools Reporting PNR" — number of FBS schools with positive net generated revenue, and median PNR among them, by fiscal year: 2005: 18 ($2.61M); 2006: 19 ($4.29M); 2007: 25 ($3.00M); 2008: 25 ($3.87M); 2009: 16 ($3.98M); 2010: 24 ($6.85M); 2011: 24 ($8.68M); 2012: 24 ($5.63M); 2013: 21 ($8.45M); 2014: 25 ($5.24M); 2015: 25 ($5.74M); 2016: 24 ($9.97M); 2017: 25 ($11.66M); 2018: 29 ($6.73M); 2019: 25 ($7.88M). — [NCAA, 15-Year Trends in Division I Athletics Finances, 2020 report (FY2005-FY2019)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- Same table extended: 2020: 20 schools ($7.38M median PNR); 2021: 9 schools ($15.77M). — [NCAA, Trends in Division I Athletics Finances, Oct. 2022 report (FY2005-FY2021)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2022RES_DI-RevExpReport_FINAL.pdf)
- FY2019 FBS: median generated revenues $61,994,000 (range $5.7M–$223.9M); median total revenues $80,900,000; median total expenses $80,814,000; median net generated revenue −$18,790,000 (range −$65.3M to +$43.7M). "Generated revenues exceeded expenses in 2019 at 25 institutions — four fewer than in 2018. The median positive net revenue for those institutions was $7.9 million." Median generated revenue +3.7% and median expenses +7.8% vs FY2018. — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- FY2019 FBS by subgroup: Autonomy (Power 5) median generated revenue $109,812,000, total expenses $120,169,000, median net generated revenue −$6,967,000; Nonautonomy (Group of 5) median generated revenue $14,226,000, total expenses $38,552,000, median net generated revenue −$22,960,000. "The median negative net generated revenue for autonomy schools was $7 million and almost $23 million for nonautonomy FBS schools." — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- FY2019 FCS: median generated revenue $4,741,000; median expenses $20,096,000; median net generated revenue −$14,316,000. "Generated revenues did not exceed expenses for any school in 2019." FY2019 D-I Subdivision (no football): median generated revenue $3,566,000; expenses $18,211,000; net −$14,397,000; no school self-sufficient. — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- FY2020 FBS: median generated revenue $61,408,176; median expenses $78,016,336; median net generated revenue −$19,305,114; A5 median −$7,758,920, nonautonomy median −$23,731,444. "Generated revenues exceeded expenses in 2020 at 20 institutions – five fewer than in 2019." FCS median −$14.8M, D-I Subdivision median −$13.85M; no FCS or subdivision school self-sufficient. — [NCAA, Trends in Division I Athletics Finances, Nov. 2021 report (FY2020)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2021RES_D1-RevExpReport.pdf)
- FY2016 (2017 report): "A total of 24 programs in the AG [Autonomy Group] showed positive net generated revenues (profits), with a median of $10,000,000, while the remaining 41 of the AG lost a median of $10,000,000; the 64 NA programs lost a median of $20,000,000; the total FBS loss is a median of $18,000,000." Median negative net generated revenue: AG $3.6M; NA $19.9M; all FBS $14.4M (the report gives both $14.4M and $18M for all-FBS — internal inconsistency in the NCAA document). — [NCAA, Revenues and Expenses 2004-2016 (2017 report)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2017D1RES_D1RevExpReportFinal.pdf)
- FY2013: "A total of 20 athletics programs in the FBS reported positive net revenues for the 2013 fiscal year"; the 103 money-losing FBS schools had a median deficit of $14.9 million; overall median loss $11.6M. — [PolitiFact, Dec. 22, 2014, citing NCAA report](https://www.politifact.com/factchecks/2014/dec/22/jim-moran/moran-says-only-20-colleges-make-profit-sports) (NOTE: NCAA's later restated trend table lists 21 FBS schools with PNR in 2013.)
- FY2006: only 19 of 119 FBS programs had positive net generated revenue; only 16 were positive in all three years 2004-06; FBS median net loss $7.265M; FBS total revenues grew 25.5%, expenses 23%, generated revenues only 16% over 2004-06; none of 118 FCS programs and none of 94 D-I non-football programs were positive. Zimbalist: "these programs on balance generate a huge deficit, and it's getting worse." — [Inside Higher Ed, Doug Lederman, May 16, 2008](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2008/05/16/money-losing-proposition)
- FY2012 (public schools only): "Just 23 of 228 athletics departments at NCAA Division I public schools generated enough money on their own to cover their expenses in 2012"; all 23 were in BCS automatic-qualifier conferences; 16 of the 23 still received some subsidy; total D-I subsidies rose nearly $200M from 2011 to 2012, the largest one-year increase since USA Today began tracking in 2004-05. — [USA Today (Berkowitz, Upton, Brady), republished by Knight Commission, June 6, 2013](https://www.knightcommission.org/2013/06/most-ncaa-division-i-athletic-departments-take-subsidies/)
- FY2010-2014 (201 public D-I schools): those departments earned $26B but spent $35B over five years; "just 21 broke even or ran a surplus," while 180 relied on $10.3B in student fees and other subsidies. — [Chronicle of Higher Education / HuffPost, "The $10-Billion Sports Tab," Nov. 2015](https://www.chronicle.com/interactives/ncaa-subsidies-main); methodology at [HuffPost reporters' note](https://projects.huffingtonpost.com/projects/ncaa/reporters-note)
- FY2014 (48 public Power Five schools): despite revenues rising from $2.6B to $4.5B since 2004, 25 of the 48 departments still ran a deficit in 2014; Hobson: "How is that possible when revenues go up year after year?" — [Poynter, Jan. 21, 2016](https://www.poynter.org/?p=392532); [SPLC summary of the Post series, Jan. 2016](https://splc.org/2016/01/post-series-college-athletic-spending-records/)
- Andrew Zimbalist (Smith College), using the NCAA's 2020 report: "only 25 of the 130 schools in the Football Bowl Subdivision (FBS) reported positive net revenues in 2019"; median operating deficits in 2019: FBS $18.8M; FCS (125 schools) $14.3M; D-I without football (94 schools) $14.4M. — [EconoFact, Jan. 22, 2023](https://econofact.org/who-wins-with-college-sports)
- Long-run FBS trend (FY2005-FY2019): "median generated revenue grew by 149% at the autonomy conference schools and by 47% at the nonautonomy schools. Median total expenses grew by 159% in autonomy schools and 92% in nonautonomy schools. In 2005, the expense gap between the median autonomy and nonautonomy schools was just over $25 million. It is now around $80 million." FY2015-FY2019: generated revenue +28% (autonomy) / +11% (nonautonomy); expenses +30% / +19%. — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- Self-sufficiency ratio (generated revenue ÷ total expenses), FY2019: median FBS ≈72%; median autonomy school "almost 100%"; median nonautonomy ≈40%; median FCS ≈27%; median D-I Subdivision ≈19%. FY2020: FBS ≈67%; A5 "over 90%"; nonautonomy ≈36%; FCS ≈23%; D-I Subdivision 16% ("the lowest of all these years"). — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf); [NCAA 2021 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2021RES_D1-RevExpReport.pdf)
- Dashboard indicators, median FBS, FY2004 vs FY2014/2015/2016: generated revenues as % of total athletic expenditures (self-sufficiency) 76.9% → 71.3% / 75.6% / 73.0%; allocated revenues as % of total revenues 21.3% → 26.5% / 25.4% / 27.0%; athletics expense per student-athlete $63,000 → $116,000 / $123,000 / $132,000 (Autonomy: $79,000 → $181,000). — [NCAA 2017 report, Table 2.3](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2017D1RES_D1RevExpReportFinal.pdf)

### Inferences
- The count of self-sufficient FBS programs was essentially flat (18–29) across 15 years even as Power 5 generated revenues rose ~149%, which is the single clearest evidence that growth in spending absorbed growth in revenue rather than closing deficits.
- The "median FBS deficit" is dominated by Group of Five schools; the Power 5 median was close to break-even on a generated-revenue basis (−$7M in FY2019), so headline "only 25 self-sufficient" figures understate how concentrated true self-sufficiency is in the Power 5.

### Gaps
- The NCAA's September 2015 press release reported 24 FBS schools positive in FY2014 (per secondary headlines), but the NCAA's later restated trend table shows 25 for 2014 and 21 for 2013 (vs. 20 in the original FY2013 report). The NCAA appears to have restated counts; the original 2015 release could not be fetched (ncaa.org redirects).
- I could not obtain the USA Today/Newhouse database's year-by-year counts of self-sufficient public schools for FY2014-FY2020 (site returned 403).

## Key Question 2: How much revenue was "allocated" (subsidy) vs. "generated," by subdivision?

### Takeaway
Across all NCAA schools roughly 44–45% of athletics revenue in FY2019–FY2020 ($8.3–8.5B) was allocated from institutions, governments and student fees; the share ranged from ~7% at the median Power 5 school to ~56–59% for Group of Five, ~71–74% for FCS and ~76–82% for non-football D-I schools.

### Cited Findings
- FY2019, all three NCAA divisions: total athletics revenues "just over $18.9 billion"; "approximately $8.3 billion (44%) was allocated revenues from government and institution support and student fees; $10.6 billion (56%) was generated by the athletics departments." D-I accounted for 96% of generated revenues; FBS autonomy schools ~72% of all generated revenues. D-I alone: $5.6 billion allocated from institution/government support and student fees. — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- FY2020: NCAA-wide revenues just over $18.7B; ~$8.5B (45%) allocated, $10.2B (55%) generated; D-I: $9.9B generated, $5.8B allocated. — [NCAA 2021 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2021RES_D1-RevExpReport.pdf)
- FY2019 generated share of total revenue by subdivision: 93% at FBS autonomy; ~44% at FBS nonautonomy; 29% at FCS; 24% at D-I Subdivision. "For the other three groups, institution and government supports is the largest revenue category: 38% for FBS nonautonomy schools, 58% for FCS, and 62% for the Division I Subdivision schools." For FBS autonomy, media rights/NCAA-conference distributions/bowl revenues are the largest category (~35%). — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- FY2020: generated revenues were 92% of total for FBS autonomy, ~41% for FBS nonautonomy, 27% for FCS, 22% for D-I Subdivision; institution/government support was 42% of nonautonomy, 60% of FCS and 64% of subdivision revenue. — [NCAA 2021 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2021RES_D1-RevExpReport.pdf)
- Allocated revenue as % of total revenue (median), FY2004 → FY2014 / 2015 / 2016: all FBS 21.3% → 26.5% / 25.4% / 27.0%; Autonomy 6% → 6% / 5% / 6%; Non-Autonomy 49% → 57% / 59% / 59%; FCS 73.8% → 72.6% / 73.7% / 74.0%; D-I without football 79.5% → 81.6% / 81.2% / 82.0%. Median allocated revenue, FY2016: Autonomy $13.73M total allocated (direct institutional $3.886M; indirect $263K; student fees $2.257M); FCS ~$12.55M (direct institutional $8.315M; student fees $1.07M); D-I w/o football ~$12.63M (direct institutional $9.216M). — [NCAA 2017 report, Table 2.3 and subdivision tables](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2017D1RES_D1RevExpReportFinal.pdf)
- Group of Five reliance: "Two funding sources under severe strain, particularly during the pandemic—student fees and institutional support –make up 56 percent of these programs' budgets" (FY2018, public G5 schools), whereas "Power 5 schools generate nearly all of their athletics revenue from external sources." — [Knight Commission, "Transforming the NCAA D-I Model," summary of first three sessions, Nov. 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/transforming-ncaa-d-i-model-summary-first-three-sessions-1120-01.pdf)
- "Institutions in the Group of 5 rely heavily on institutional support to fund their athletics budgets. The median support from institutional funds and student fees was approximately $23 million, with median institutional support jumping 18 percent in a single year between 2018 to 2019." — [Knight Commission, Transforming the NCAA D-I Model: Recommendations for Change, Dec. 3, 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-recommendations-for-change-1220-01.pdf)
- NCAA 5-year trend: "During the most recent five-year period [FY2015-19], support coming from the institution in the form of direct allocation or student fees actually decreased by 9% at the median autonomy school. Nonautonomy schools saw an 18% increase at the median." — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- Knight CAFI chart, 109 public FBS schools, FY2018 (chart values read from slide text; approximate): for all FBS publics, student fees ≈7% and institutional/government support ≈10% of revenue; for FBS expense quartile 1 (budgets $117M–$207M), student fees and institutional support each <1%; for quartile 4 (budgets $16M–$36M), student fees ≈24% and institutional/government support ≈39%; FCS publics: student fees ≈25%, institutional/government support ≈44%; non-football D-I publics: student fees ≈35%, institutional/government support ≈40%. — [Knight Commission, Session 1 slide deck, Sept. 16, 2020 (data: 2017-18 NCAA financial reports)](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/kcia-transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-session-1-slide-deck-091620-01.pdf)
- Budget ranges FY2018 (public D-I): FBS $16M–$207M (109 schools); FCS $4M–$52M (76); non-football $4M–$35M (46). FY2020: FBS $18M–$215M; FCS $4M–$58M. — [Knight Session 1 deck](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/kcia-transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-session-1-slide-deck-091620-01.pdf); [Knight Commission, 2020 D-I Finances graphs (2019-20 NCAA reports)](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/2020DIFinancesGraphs.pdf)
- Student fees (Washington Post, FY2014, 52 public Power Five schools): students at 32 schools paid a combined $125.5 million in athletic fees; per-capita fees included Virginia $657 ($13.2M of $70.5M athletics revenue), Maryland $406 ($11.3M), Rutgers $326 ($10.3M of $40.3M), N.C. State $328, Virginia Tech $288, UNC $279, Georgia Tech $254, Florida State $237, Auburn $192, Utah $171; Texas A&M's president proposed charging all 50,000 students $72/year to help pay for a $450M stadium renovation. — [Washington Post (Hobson & Rich), Nov. 30, 2015, as reproduced by Academe Blog, Dec. 9, 2015](https://academeblog.org/2015/12/09/more-on-academic-and-student-subsidizing-of-intercollegiate-athletics/)
- Chronicle/HuffPost: Georgia State students paid nearly $90M in mandatory athletic fees over five years (FY2010-14); Rutgers, Northern Illinois and Georgia State relied on subsidies for more than half of operating expenses; "schools that subsidize sports the most also tend to have the poorest students." — [Chronicle "The $10-Billion Sports Tab"](https://www.chronicle.com/interactives/ncaa-subsidies-main); [HuffPost reporters' note](https://projects.huffingtonpost.com/projects/ncaa/reporters-note)
- Rutgers received nearly $47M in subsidies from the university for 2012-13 (USA Today data). — [Washington Monthly, Feb. 28, 2014](https://washingtonmonthly.com/2014/02/28/how-much-money-does-the-rutgers-sports-program-need/)
- Delta Cost Project (public D-I, FY2010): "Very few Division I athletic departments are self-funded; instead, most programs rely on athletic subsidies from institutions and students"; median subsidies ran "nearly $20,000 to $30,000 per athlete in each subdivision—exceeding the median overall educational spending per student"; "In each of the subdivisions, subsidies rose nearly as fast as athletic spending between 2005 and 2010, suggesting that institutions themselves have contributed to the rise in athletic spending." In 2010 more than 80% of the typical FBS budget came from generated revenue vs. more than 70% from subsidies at FCS/non-football schools. — [Desrochers, "Academic Spending Versus Athletic Spending: Who Wins?", Delta Cost Project/AIR, Jan. 2013](https://www.air.org/sites/default/files/2021-06/Academic-Spending-vs-Athletic-Spending.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "deficit" problem is bimodal: the Power 5 median is close to self-sufficient but spends to the edge of its revenue; the rest of D-I is structurally dependent on allocated money, and the dependence of Group of Five schools rose over FY2015-19 while the Power 5's fell.

## Key Question 3: Specific cost drivers (quantified)

### Takeaway
Pre-2021 spending growth was concentrated in three areas the Knight Commission identified from CAFI data — coaching salaries (Power 5 football coaching pay +93% real, 2009-18), non-coaching administrative staff (+69% payroll at 48 Power Five schools 2004-14), and debt-financed facilities ($9.2B FBS public debt, $578M/yr Power 5 debt service) — layered on top of scholarship costs, cost-of-attendance stipends (2015), and a broad-based sports portfolio in which every sport other than football and men's basketball loses money at the median.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Coaching salaries and buyouts**
- "Data show these revenue increases have disproportionately fueled spending in three areas: coaching salaries, non-coaching administrative positions and salaries, and athletics facilities expenditures." Power 5 football coaching salaries "doubled from 2009-2018 to an average of nearly $1 million for each of the team's 10 permitted FBS coaches... By comparison, the financial value of the average grant-in-aid provided to each Power 5 athlete on scholarship was $23,883." — [Knight Commission summary, Nov. 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/transforming-ncaa-d-i-model-summary-first-three-sessions-1120-01.pdf)
- 51 Power 5 public institutions, FY2009-FY2018, inflation-adjusted: football coaches' salaries up 93% to $496M; all other coaches' salaries up 60% to $624M; athletic student aid up 49% to $698M. Group of 5 publics: football coaches' salaries up 61% to $167M (550 coaches); other coaches up 34% to $247M (2,083 coaches); student aid up 51% to $439M (23,682 athletes). — [Knight Commission Session 1 slide deck, Sept. 16, 2020 (CAFI + EADA data)](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/kcia-transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-session-1-slide-deck-091620-01.pdf)
- NCAA: coaches' compensation as a share of total expenses at autonomy schools rose "from about 16% in 2005 to over 19% in 2019" and to 20% in FY2020; total compensation ~36% of autonomy expenses in FY2019 (a 4-point rise), ~38% in FY2020. D-I spent ~$3.0B on coaches' compensation in FY2019 (19% of $15.7B D-I expenses, the largest category) and ~$3.1B in FY2020. — [NCAA 2020 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf); [NCAA 2021 report](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2021RES_D1-RevExpReport.pdf)
- USA Today 2019 database: average FBS head football coach pay $2.67M (122 of 130 coaches), up 9% from 2018, "largest in four years"; SEC average $4.95M, first conference where every coach made ≥$3M. — [FootballScoop on USA Today 2019 database](https://footballscoop.com/news/usa-today-released-2019-coaches-salaries-database/); [Sports Business Daily, Oct. 23, 2019](https://www.sportsbusinessdaily.com/Daily/Issues/2019/10/23/Colleges/CFB-Salaries.aspx)
- USA Today 2012: average FBS head coach $1.64M, up 12% year over year and "more than a 70% jump since 2006." — [SI Wire, Nov. 20, 2012](https://www.si.com/si-wire/2012/11/20/college-football-coaches-salaries-remain-high)
- Charles Clotfelter (Duke): football coach salaries rose by a factor of 7.5 between 1986 and 2010 while professor compensation rose 32% (reported from a 2011 talk). — [Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/?p=1045411); book: [Clotfelter, Big-Time Sports in American Universities (Cambridge, 2011; 2nd ed. 2018)](https://econ.duke.edu/books/big-time-sports-american-universities)
- Supreme Court (Gorsuch, majority): "annual salaries for top Division I college football coaches approach $11 million"; "The president of the NCAA earns nearly $4 million per year"; "Commissioners of the top conferences take home between $2 to $5 million"; "College athletic directors average more than $1 million annually"; "By 2016, NCAA Division I schools raised more than $13.5 billion." Kavanaugh (concurring): "Colleges build lavish new facilities." — [NCAA v. Alston, 594 U.S. ___ (June 21, 2021)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-512)
- Buyouts/"dead money": public FBS schools paid more than $533.6 million in dead money to fired head and assistant coaches in football and men's/women's basketball from Jan. 1, 2010 to Jan. 31, 2021 (86 of 130 FBS programs provided records; 22 private schools excluded). Power 5 = 88% of total: football $354.9M, men's basketball $103.5M, women's basketball $12.5M; by conference SEC $151M, Pac-12 $114.1M, Big Ten $106.8M, Big 12 $58.8M, ACC $40.0M; Group of 5 $62.8M. Top schools: Auburn $31.2M, Nebraska $25.8M, Texas $21.5M, Ole Miss $20.4M, Kansas $20M. Auburn's Gus Malzahn buyout >$21M (Dec. 2020). Amy Perko (Knight Commission): "hundreds of millions of dollars will just continue to be wasted." — [ESPN (Paula Lavigne & Mark Schlabach), Nov. 5, 2021, republished by ABC/GMA](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/living/story/fbs-schools-spent-5336-million-dead-money-10-80990044)
- USA Today's 2017 database added a buyout column; Jimbo Fisher's Texas A&M buyout was $68.125M, highest listed. — [FootballScoop on 2017 USA Today database](https://www.footballscoop.com/2017-fbs-head-coaches-salary-database-released)

**(b) Facilities "arms race" and debt service**
- "As of 2018, FBS public schools had more than $9.2 billion in athletics debt, with the vast majority of that debt ($7.4 billion) shared among the 52 Power 5 public institutions for whom data is publicly available. These 52 institutions alone pay a combined $578 million in annual debt service on these long-term financial commitments." Group of 5 (55 publics): $1.8B. — [Knight Commission summary, Nov. 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/transforming-ncaa-d-i-model-summary-first-three-sessions-1120-01.pdf); [Knight Session 1 deck](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/kcia-transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-session-1-slide-deck-091620-01.pdf)
- Bond Buyer (2020), citing Knight Commission: the 10 largest D-I conferences carried over $9.5B in debt on major athletic facilities with annual debt service and leases near $1.5B. — [Bond Buyer, 2020](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/coronavirus-challenges-stadium-bonds-as-college-football-seasons-stall)
- Washington Post: 48 public Power Five schools "spent $772 million combined on athletic facilities" in 2014, "an 89-percent increase from $408 million spent in 2004, adjusted for inflation." ### Inferences

## Key Question 4: What did economists and commissions say about the structural incentive to spend?

### Takeaway
The Knight Commission (2010, 2020), Delta Cost Project (2013), Zimbalist, Clotfelter and the Drake Group all describe a non-profit "arms race" in which unrestricted revenue is spent in pursuit of wins rather than retained; Rodney Fort offers a dissenting principal-agent explanation that locates the cause in university budgeting rather than inter-school rivalry.

### Cited Findings
- Knight Commission, "Restoring the Balance" (June 2010): spending in big-time college sports grew 38% from 2005 to 2008, nearly twice the growth in academic spending; median athletics spending per athlete rose 38% to $84,446 while academic spending per student rose ~20% to $13,349; FBS schools spent 6.3 times more per athlete than per student in 2008; key recommendation was greater financial transparency. — [Knight Commission press release, June 17, 2010](https://www.knightcommission.org/2010/06/knight-commission-calls-for-college-sports-reform-recommends-public-transparency-of-finances-and-new-financial-incentives/); [Knight media summary](https://www.knightcommission.org/2010/07/summary-of-media-coverage-of-restoring-the-balance/)
- Delta Cost Project (Desrochers, Jan. 2013, public D-I, FY2005-2010): "Athletic departments spend far more per athlete than institutions spend to educate the average student—typically three to six times as much; among FBS institutions, median athletic spending was nearly $92,000 per athlete in 2010, while median academic spending per FTE student was less than $14,000." "Athletic costs increased at least twice as fast as academic spending, on a per-capita basis across each of the three Division I subdivisions." FBS quartile 1 spent $149,711 per athlete (9.1× academic spending per student); median FBS $91,936 (6.7×); quartile 4 $51,532 (4.4×). 2005-2010 (current dollars): athletic spending per athlete +41.5% to +59.5% by quartile vs academic +17% to +30%; athletic subsidy per athlete +16.5% to +57.8%. Six BCS conferences' median per-athlete spending topped $100,000; SEC median nearly four times the Sun Belt's (<$42,000). Compensation ≈ one third of budgets, coaching salaries ≈ half of that; "increases in facilities and equipment have consumed a larger piece of the spending pie over time across all subdivisions." — [Desrochers, Delta Cost Project/AIR](https://www.air.org/sites/default/files/2021-06/Academic-Spending-vs-Athletic-Spending.pdf)
- Knight Commission, Dec. 3, 2020: "For the relatively small number of revenue-rich Power 5 schools... the problem is that expenses are growing even faster than revenues. According to an NCAA report, from 2004 to 2019, median revenues generated from sources external to the institution rose 149 percent, only to be outpaced by expenses, which rose 159 percent." "Even prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, many Division I athletic programs were hemorrhaging money, raising student fees to inappropriate levels, spending beyond their means on coaching salaries and gilded facilities to keep up with FBS competitors, while shortchanging or even dropping Olympic sports." Survey of D-I leaders (June-July 2020): nearly 60% of FBS respondents said they overspend in football to keep up with better-resourced teams; 67% favored an antitrust exemption to control costs (>80% of Power 5 respondents); 62% favored conference-level caps on operating budgets including coaching salaries. — [Knight Commission, Transforming the NCAA D-I Model: Recommendations for Change](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-recommendations-for-change-1220-01.pdf); [Knight summary, Nov. 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/transforming-ncaa-d-i-model-summary-first-three-sessions-1120-01.pdf)
- Knight Commission Session 1 "Key Points" (Sept. 2020): "Trends for Power 5 conferences prior to pandemic demonstrate disproportionate increases in football coaching salaries, administrative staff and facilities expenditures (debt service). Pandemic will have huge impact on FY20 and FY21 due to lack of reserves." — [Knight Session 1 deck](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/kcia-transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-session-1-slide-deck-091620-01.pdf)
- Nancy Zimpher (Knight Commission member): "A post-pandemic model for college sports should address excessive spending and promote fiscal sanity..." — [Knight summary, Nov. 2020](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/transforming-ncaa-d-i-model-summary-first-three-sessions-1120-01.pdf)
- Andrew Zimbalist (early 2000s, NCWGE briefing): "the financial problem with college sports today is not Title IX... the problem is waste"; the average D-IA deficit was $600,000 in 2001, but adding $1.425M in student fees and $4.625M in donations yields a "standalone athletic department operating deficit" averaging $6.05M; stakeholders "who demand victory of a not-for-profit entity" mean that "when an additional revenue stream is projected for athletics, ADs easily find a way to spend it in the pursuit of victory." Athletic departments are "large, not-for-profit bureaucracies" that "follow the bureaucratic imperative to grow at any cost and to do anything they can to win." — [Zimbalist, NCWGE Briefing Paper No. 4](https://fbaum.unc.edu/lobby/_107th/135_Title%20IX/Organizational_Statements/NCWGE/NCWGE_Press_Briefing_Paper_4.pdf)
- Zimbalist (2023): "Athletic departments answer to boosters, alumni, and administrators who want wins, not profits"; in 2019, 35% of D-I's $15.8B went to administrative and coach compensation, 18% to facilities, 18.2% ($2.9B) to scholarships, 1% to medical; the median FBS head football coach earns above $3.5M. — [EconoFact, Jan. 22, 2023](https://econofact.org/who-wins-with-college-sports)
- Clotfelter: "rather than being the inconsequential student activity that universities often imply that it is, big-time sport has become a core function of the universities that engage in it"; athletic budgets "have ballooned" as cable TV and commercial opportunities proliferated. — [Duke Economics book page, Big-Time Sports in American Universities](https://econ.duke.edu/books/big-time-sports-american-universities)
- Bowen's "revenue theory of cost" (Howard Bowen, 1980): institutions "raise as much money as they possibly can and then spend nearly the entirety of it"; NCAA-commissioned reports by economists (2003, 2005, 2009, Orszag et al.) "basically said each new dollar of college sports expense goes hand in hand with a new dollar of revenue." — [Wikipedia, Revenue theory of cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenue_theory_of_cost); [FiveThirtyEight, "The NCAA Isn't Going Broke No Matter How Much You Hear It"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/the-ncaa-isnt-going-broke-no-matter-how-much-you-hear-it) (article could not be fetched directly; summary from search results)
- Dissent: Rodney Fort (Michigan), "College Athletics Spending: Principals and Agents v. Arms Race," Journal of Amateur Sport 2(1), 2016, pp. 119-140: the widely held view that ADs are "trapped in an expenditure arms race" "completely omits the actual consideration of the university budgeting process"; he proposes a principal-agent model between university administrators and athletic departments that "fits the aggregate data" with different policy implications. — [Journal of Amateur Sport](https://journals.ku.edu/jams/article/view/5673)
- Washington Post series (Hobson & Rich, Nov. 23–Dec. 29, 2015), "Running Up the Bills": ADs defended spending by saying their programs bring benefits not shown on athletics statements; Ramogi Huma (NCPA) called it the product of "an illegal market" that suppresses player benefits. — [Washington Post, Nov. 23, 2015](http://www.washingtonpost.com/sf/sports/wp/2015/11/23/running-up-the-bills/) (paywalled; details via [SPLC](https://splc.org/2016/01/post-series-college-athletic-spending-records/) and [NCPA republication](https://ncpanow.org/news-articles/as-college-sports-revenues-spike-coaches-are-not-only-ones-cashing-in))
- Anne D. Neal (ACTA, 2013): institutions are "always trying to play catch-up in the athletic realm," which "raises questions as to whether institutions are paying attention to their primary purpose, which is education." — [Knight Commission/USA Today, June 2013](https://www.knightcommission.org/2013/06/most-ncaa-division-i-athletic-departments-take-subsidies/)
- Drake Group (Dec. 2020) criticized the Knight proposal as "devoid of any specificity" on athlete education outcomes and for "sacrificing 16,000 FBS football players" to a separate football entity. — [Drake Group, Dec. 12, 2020](https://www.thedrakegroup.org/2020/12/12/failing-to-confront-the-elephants-in-the-room-the-drake-group-comments-on-the-knight-commission-proposal-to-transform-the-ncaa-d-i-model/)

### Inferences
- The consensus mechanism is Bowen-style: unrestricted media/CFP revenue plus no shareholders and a win-maximizing principal (boosters/trustees) means surpluses are converted into coaching pay, staff and facilities; Fort's critique implies the university (not the AD) chooses the subsidy level, which supports the "deficits are a choice" rebuttal in Q6.

- Could not obtain verbatim passages from Clotfelter's book or Jeff Smith's "Unsportsmanlike Conduct"; no GAO report or congressional testimony specifically on pre-2021 deficits was located in this pass.

## Key Question 5: How did COVID (FY2020/FY2021) affect deficits, and how many programs cut sports before NIL?

### Takeaway
FY2020 (ending June 30, 2020) captured only the first months of the shutdown — FBS median generated revenue fell just 1% and 20 schools stayed positive — but FY2021 saw FBS median generated revenue fall 43% and only 9 FBS schools break even; roughly 110 D-I teams were cut in 2020-21 (86 after reinstatements), with several schools (Stanford, Cal, WSU) explicitly citing deficits that predated the pandemic.

### Cited Findings
- FY2020 (NCAA): FBS median generated revenues −1%, median total expenses −3.5% vs FY2019; 20 FBS schools positive (vs 25); FCS median generated revenue −9.3% ("consecutive drop in 3 years, but 2020 is the biggest drop"); D-I Subdivision median generated revenue −22%; athletics expenses at both autonomy and nonautonomy schools grew 5 points slower than institutional expenses. — [NCAA 2021 report (FY2020)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2021RES_D1-RevExpReport.pdf)
- FY2020, 52 public Power 5 schools: total operating revenue fell from ~$6.7B to ~$6.5B (−$205M; average −$3.9M per school; 33 of 52 reported lower revenue); expenses fell from ~$6.56B to ~$6.41B (−$142M; 39 of 52 lower); average net result fell from ~$3.1M to ~$1.9M; "FY2020 includes the 2019 college football season and only the first few months of the shutdown." — [Andy Wittry, Out of Bounds, June 8, 2021](https://andywittry.substack.com/p/power-5-financial-reports-from-2020)
- Penn State FY2020: $165,077,390 net revenues vs $157,908,311 expenses, +$7.17M. — [Penn State Athletics, May 28, 2021](https://gopsusports.com/news/2021/05/28/penn-state-athletics-remains-self-supporting)
- FY2021 (NCAA): FBS median generated revenue −43%, median total expenses −17%; median net generated revenue −$22.6M; autonomy median −$18.2M, nonautonomy −$23.6M ("the difference... was the lowest in the 17 years studied"); "Generated revenue exceeded expenses in 2021 at just nine FBS institutions, the lowest in the 17 years studied"; FCS median generated revenue −31.7%. — [NCAA 2022 report (FY2021)](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2022RES_DI-RevExpReport_FINAL.pdf); [Front Office Sports (Amanda Christovich), Oct. 20, 2022](https://frontofficesports.com/article/fbs-median-revenue-dropped-43-during-pandemic/) (FBS median revenue $35M, expenses ~$65M in 2020-21; Power 5 spent 42% of revenue on coaching and administrative salaries)
- Sports cut: "110 Division I sports teams have been discontinued in the 2020–21 cycle. But 24 of those were eventually reinstated... That leaves 86 eliminated athletic teams, the most cut since 1997–98" (102). Stanford cut 11 teams affecting 240 athletes; pre-COVID the department faced a deficit "projected at 'at least $12 million this year'"; the cuts were to save $8M annually. Winston & Strawn's Jeffrey Kessler: "It's really shocking to see a school like Stanford cut these sports since that school is richer than Midas." — [Sports Illustrated (Dellenger), Feb. 12, 2021](https://www.si.com/college/2021/02/12/stanford-save-cut-sports-movement-ncaa)

- Iowa (Aug. 2020) dropped men's gymnastics, men's tennis and men's and women's swimming & diving citing a projected $100M revenue loss. — [AP via FOX6, Aug. 2020](https://fox6now.com/sports/iowa-hawkeyes-drop-4-sports-amid-financial-crisis-tied-to-virus)

## Key Question 6: Rebuttals — is the "deficit" an accounting artifact or a design choice?

### Takeaway
Critics (Schwarz, Rascher) argue reported deficits overstate true losses because scholarships are booked at sticker price and many institutional benefits are not credited, while the NCAA's own data and Washington Post reporting show that even rich departments budget to spend essentially everything they take in — making "deficit" partly a function of institutional choice to subsidize and of the non-profit's incentive to spend to zero.

### Cited Findings
- NCAA self-sufficiency definition excludes allocated revenue by construction: a department is self-sufficient only if "generated operating revenues — not counting money from student fees, university funding or direct government support — are at least equal to its total operating expenses." — [NCAA 2020 report definitions](https://ncaaorg.s3.amazonaws.com/research/Finances/2020RES_D1-RevExp_Report.pdf)
- Knight CAFI: Power 5 public revenues up 62% to $6.42B and expenses up 60% to $6.22B (FY2009-18); summary text: "Expenses increased in lockstep with massive revenue growth." — [Knight Session 1 deck](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/kcia-transforming-the-ncaa-d-i-model-session-1-slide-deck-091620-01.pdf); [Knight summary](https://www.knightcommission.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/transforming-ncaa-d-i-model-summary-first-three-sessions-1120-01.pdf)
- Washington Post: 48 Power Five publics' revenue grew from $2.6B to $4.5B since 2004 "but spending has grown proportionately right along with it"; Florida State, UCLA, Auburn and Wisconsin spent more than they brought in despite strong teams. — [SPLC summary](https://splc.org/2016/01/post-series-college-athletic-spending-records/)
- Accounting choices in NCAA reports: Georgia's reported $40M FY2020 surplus "did not reflect more than $21 million in construction payments or an annual $4.5 million payment to the university"; Alabama deferred $24.5M in contributions from FY2019 to FY2020, turning a $21.2M "loss" into a $16.1M gain; Maryland and Rutgers reported $0 media-rights revenue (booking it as conference distributions) while Wisconsin reported >$50M; NC State covered a $2.1M loss from reserves. — [Andy Wittry, June 8, 2021](https://andywittry.substack.com/p/power-5-financial-reports-from-2020)
- Andy Schwarz (economist): schools treat "the full sticker price charged to a non-aid student as the real cost of adding one more athlete," which he calls the primary reason schools err in valuing a scholarship; at UNM he estimated true savings from cutting sports at ~$300,000 vs the university's $1.1M claim; at Hartford he identified a $6.2M "double count error" in merit-aid accounting. — [Daily Lobo, Sept. 2018](https://www.dailylobo.com/article/2018/09/economist-brief-unm-sports-cuts); [CT Mirror, May 17, 2021](https://ctmirror.org/2021/05/17/the-not-so-spun-truth-about-the-university-of-hartford/)
- Daniel Rascher: departments understate revenue and overstate expenses by not counting general donations, university-branded merchandise, or athletics' effect on applications/enrollment; at Sonoma State he estimated athletics' net financial impact at +$2.2M to +$4.4M annually. — [Sonoma State Star](https://sonomastatestar.com/?p=37961)
- Counterpoint: the NCAA and the Knight Commission both include grants-in-aid as an athletics expense, and an NCAA research consultant (Transylvania University accounting professor) said scholarships should be included in the bottom line. — [CT Mirror, May 17, 2021](https://ctmirror.org/2021/05/17/the-not-so-spun-truth-about-the-university-of-hartford/)
- Cal's spokesman argued the stadium debt "has little if any impact on that deficit" because the stadium itself showed a surplus — an argument the Chronicle's columnist said "only works if you divorce the stadium loan from the athletic department deficit altogether." — [SF Chronicle via PressReader, Aug. 22, 2017](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/san-francisco-chronicle-late-edition/20170822/281775629279005)
- Rutgers' fringe-benefit rate (72.9% in FY2025, set by New Jersey) and NCAA-report conventions (salary lines include fringe; schools file expenses differently) make cross-school comparisons unreliable; Rutgers' cumulative deficit since joining the Big Ten in 2014-15 was $516.9M. — [Extra Points (Matt Brown), Feb. 3, 2026](https://www.extrapointsmb.com/p/wait-why-is-rutgers-so-dang-broke)
- Delta Cost Project on benefits that don't appear on athletics statements: "evidence of the ancillary benefits of college sports is mixed"; application bumps are "typically quite modest," "primarily associated with success in football," and last "only a year or two"; "Most of the recent studies on alumni giving find little connection between athletic success and fundraising." — [Desrochers, Delta Cost Project](https://www.air.org/sites/default/files/2021-06/Academic-Spending-vs-Athletic-Spending.pdf)
- Fort (2016): the arms-race story "omits the actual consideration of the university budgeting process" — i.e., the university, as principal, chooses the subsidy. — [Journal of Amateur Sport](https://journals.ku.edu/jams/article/view/5673)
- Ohio State pattern (post-period illustration of spend-to-revenue): FY2023 revenue $279.5M vs expenses $274.9M; FY2024 ~$255M revenue vs ~$292M expenses (−$38M); FY2025 $336.1M vs $320.4M. Ohio State was one of 24 self-sufficient public departments in FY2014 per USA Today. — [Eleven Warriors, Sept. 2015](https://www.elevenwarriors.com/college-sports/2015/09/59779/report-ohio-state-one-of-24-self-sufficient-public-school-athletic-departments); [Buckeye Sports Bulletin FY2023](https://buckeyesports.com/ohio-state-reports-record-athletics-revenue-nearly-280-million-for-2023-fiscal-year/); [The Lantern FY2024](https://www.thelantern.com/?p=370251); [Buckeye Sports Bulletin FY2025](https://www.buckeyesports.com/ohio-state-sets-record-for-revenue-in-2025-fiscal-year)

### Inferences
- Both camps agree on the underlying fact pattern: revenue is spent nearly in full. The dispute is whether to call the resulting gap a "deficit" (NCAA/Knight/USA Today framing) or a chosen institutional investment with mis-measured marginal costs (Schwarz/Rascher/Fort). For the report, the safest formulation is that pre-NIL departments were structurally designed to spend to zero, with most needing allocated money to do so.

- No neutral peer-reviewed study was found that re-estimates D-I deficits under alternative (marginal-cost) scholarship accounting.
