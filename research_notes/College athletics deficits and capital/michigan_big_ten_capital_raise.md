# Why Michigan Opposed the Big Ten's $2.4B "Big Ten Enterprises" / UC Investments Capital Deal (vs. Ohio State and Penn State support)

Research date: Oct. 22, 2026. Note on recency: repeated searches (standard and extended) for 2026 developments returned nothing after mid-January 2026 that reports a vote, a revival, a restructured deal, or a formal abandonment. Every substantive source below is dated Jan. 2025 – Jan. 2026. The state of play as of Oct. 2026 is therefore best described as "paused since Nov. 17, 2025, with no public revival reported" (see Status section and Gaps).

## Key Question 1: Deal terms — what was proposed, how proceeds would be distributed, vote threshold, and whether the vote was delayed

### Takeaway
UC Investments (the University of California's ~$190B pension/endowment investment office) offered ~$2.4B for a 10% stake in a new for-profit subsidiary, "Big Ten Enterprises," that would hold the league's media and sponsorship rights, implying a $24B valuation; the price was a 10-year grant-of-rights extension (2036→2046), tiered upfront payments ($190M to Michigan/Ohio State/Penn State, ~$145–150M to USC/Oregon, $100–110M to the other 13) and a permanently uneven annual split (5.5% / 5% / 4.9%, with 10% to UC). The deal required unanimity of all 18 schools; a vote floated for Nov. 21, 2025 never happened because UC Investments paused the deal on Nov. 17, 2025.

**Core economic terms (as reported)**
- $2.4B in one-time equity for a 10% minority stake in Big Ten Enterprises plus a share of annual distributions; implied $24B valuation; potential 20-year agreement during which the league may renegotiate media rights up to three times; average upfront ~$140M per school, every school at least $100M, several above $150M; "eight-figure bonuses" expected in FY2037 when the FOX deal ends. — [Yahoo Sports, Dellenger, Oct. 10, 2025](https://sports.yahoo.com/college-football/article/big-ten-nearing-decision-on-24-billion-deal-with-california-pension-investment-fund-in-landmark-move-within-college-athletics-170051033.html)
- Upfront tiers: Michigan, Ohio State and Penn State up to $190M each (~$40–50M more than USC and Oregon); the other 13 schools $100–110M; annual share: Michigan/Ohio State/Penn State ~5.5% of league revenue, others 5% or less; a performance mechanism (ACC "success initiative"-style) would let schools that win in football/basketball earn a higher rate. — [Yahoo Sports, Dellenger, Nov. 9, 2025](https://sports.yahoo.com/college-football/breaking-news/article/sources-big-ten-execs-pressing-to-make-24-billion-investment-deal--without-michigan-and-usc-if-needed-140045573.html)

**Big Ten Enterprises structure / governance**
- A private, for-profit offshoot to consolidate league and school assets (ticket sales, sponsorships, etc.), led by a CEO reporting to a board of member-school representatives; UC expected to hold a seat on a board of possibly 10; BTE "may eventually become involved in athlete compensation." — [Yahoo Sports, Dellenger, Oct. 10, 2025](https://sports.yahoo.com/college-football/article/big-ten-nearing-decision-on-24-billion-deal-with-california-pension-investment-fund-in-landmark-move-within-college-athletics-170051033.html)

### Inferences
- The unanimity requirement was imposed by the investor's terms (UC wanted all 18 bound through 2046), which gave Michigan and USC an effective veto; the Nov. 9 "16-school" workaround was the league's attempt to route around that veto and appears to have provoked the harshest Michigan reaction (Bernstein's "strong-arm" accusation came days later).
- The tiered split means the deal was not primarily a "rich schools subsidize poor schools" transfer in the upfront money — the opposite. Michigan would have been a top-tier beneficiary and still opposed it, which supports the view that its objection was about control, duration and principle rather than its own cash share.

## Key Question 2: Michigan's position — statements, reasons, political and institutional context

### Takeaway
Michigan's eight publicly elected regents took a unanimous public position against the deal on Oct. 16, 2025 (Bernstein: a "payday loan," "reckless and short-sighted"; Acker: "You can't borrow your way out of a spending problem") and reaffirmed it Nov. 20, 2025 ("We remain opposed to this deal" while "committed to the Big Ten"); the stated reasons were selling equity in a public asset, the 2046 lock-in, lack of information/rushed process, that the money doesn't fix the cost problem, and governance of BTE — plus, by mid-November, open anger at Petitti for allegedly threatening penalties and trying to proceed without Michigan.

### Inferences
- Michigan's opposition is best read as a layered objection: (1) principle (don't sell equity in a public asset / "not selling off our equity"), (2) duration (2046 lock-in forecloses optionality, including a super league or independence after 2036), (3) diagnosis (the money papers over a cost/spending problem rather than fixing it), (4) governance (BTE weighted voting and local control over night games/signage/sponsorship), and (5) process (board bypassed, rushed timeline, perceived coercion). The cash share was not the issue — Michigan was in the top tier.

## Key Question 5: Why the divergence — financial, governance, philosophical and structural explanations

### Takeaway
The split tracks (a) who most needed cash now (Penn State's buyout/stadium/House costs; Ohio State's push for a bigger permanent share) versus who could absorb a ~$15–27M House-era shortfall through cuts (Michigan); (b) governance — Michigan's elected, partisan, publicly meeting regents versus appointed boards elsewhere that were told the decision was "nonvoting" for them; and (c) Michigan's stated principle that a one-time lump sum for a permanent 10% revenue give-up and a 2046 lock-in is a "payday loan" — a position it held even though it stood to receive the largest check.

### Inferences
- The most parsimonious explanation for the split is not cash need alone (Michigan had a deficit too) but the interaction of (i) Michigan's ability to close its gap internally, (ii) a governing board that is elected, public and willing to defy the commissioner, and (iii) an institutional preference for retaining optionality through 2036. Ohio State and Penn State had appointed boards that deferred to presidents, larger immediate liabilities (Penn State) or an explicit desire for a bigger permanent share (Ohio State), and presidents who kept the decision inside the Council of Presidents and Chancellors.
- The fact that Michigan rejected the largest check is the strongest evidence that the objection was structural (equity sale + lock-in + governance) rather than distributional; USC's objection, by contrast, was substantially distributional.

### Gaps
- I found no source documenting Michigan's historical commercial conservatism (e.g., alcohol sales, jersey ads, BTN) as a cited reason in 2025 coverage; that framing would be the report writer's inference, not a sourced claim.
- No comparative data on Ohio State's or Penn State's athletic debt loads tied to the deal were surfaced beyond the Beaver Stadium $700M figure.
